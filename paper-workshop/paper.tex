\title{A Drift-Robust E-Process for Nested Forecast Comparison}

\author{Anonymous}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Tests of out-of-sample predictive ability compare a model against a benchmark, and handle the
outcome's unconditional drift by plugging in an estimate of it. When the benchmark is the zero
forecast, the resulting statistic loads on that drift: we show by simulation that Clark--West
rejects a \emph{pure-noise} signal $81\%$ of the time at the nominal $5\%$ level once the asset
drifts at an annualised Sharpe ratio of $2$. We give an e-process that removes the drift
instead of estimating it, by taking an infimum over the nuisance of a convex combination of a
signal-betting martingale and a drift-centring martingale. Ville's inequality then bounds the
rejection probability at $\alpha$ in finite samples, uniformly in time and uniformly in the
unknown drift; measured, it never exceeds $1\%$ across the same sweep. We report what the
construction costs rather than only what it fixes: against a correctly specified Clark--West
test it needs roughly twice the sample at matched size, and anytime-validity itself accounts
for a factor of $1.90$. Inverting the process over its value parameter yields a time-uniform
\emph{upper} bound on incremental predictive value, which is available whether or not anything
is rejected. Applied to a century of equity-premium data it certifies nothing, and the
median of its marginal $95\%$ upper endpoints on incremental annualised information ratio is
$0.60$.
\end{abstract}

\section{The failure this fixes}

\paragraph{Context.} E-values and test martingales
\citep{ville1939,shafer2019,grunwald2024,ramdas2023,waudbysmith2024} give anytime-valid
inference with merging rules \citep{vovk2021} and false-discovery control \citep{wang2022} that
hold under arbitrary dependence. They are not yet used in the forecast-evaluation literature,
where the standard toolkit remains fixed-sample. This note supplies the missing construction
for one specific and consequential composite null, and reports its price.


Let $y_t$ be an outcome and $g_t$ a predictable signal. A forecast-comparison test asks whether
$g_t$ carries information about $y_t$ beyond a benchmark. Under nesting the Diebold--Mariano
statistic is invalid, because the loss differential is degenerate under the null
\citep{clark2001}; Clark--West \citep{clark2007} supplies the standard adjustment and is what
the applied literature uses.

Both handle the outcome's unconditional mean by estimating it. That is the weak point. Table
\ref{tab:drift} sweeps the drift of an asset whose returns are \emph{independent of the
signal}, so every rejection is a false one. Against a zero benchmark, Clark--West's rejection
rate rises with the drift and reaches $0.81$; the statistic is rewarding the asset for having
gone up. Against an estimated-mean benchmark it is calibrated, but that repair costs the
estimate's own error and still gives no guarantee at a data-dependent stopping time.

\section{Construction}

Let $\mathcal{F}_t$ be the filtration generated by everything observable through period $t$,
and let $g_t$ be $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$-measurable. The null is composite in the drift,
\[
  H_0:\quad \mathbb{E}\!\left[\,y_t-\mu \,\middle|\, \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]=0
  \quad\text{for some fixed but unknown } \mu\in\mathbb{R},
\]
so the signal carries no information about the outcome, whatever the outcome's mean.

Fix a candidate $\mu$ and put $\psi_t(\mu)=(y_t-\mu)/\hat\sigma_t$, with $\hat\sigma_t$ the
running scale through $t-1$. Define two non-negative martingales,
\begin{align*}
  W^{\mathrm{sig}}_t(\mu) &= \textstyle\prod_{s\le t}\bigl(1+\lambda_s\,g_s\,\psi_s(\mu)\bigr),
  &\lambda_s &\in \bigl[0,\ c/(|g_s|E)\bigr] \ \text{predictable},\\
  W^{\mathrm{ctr}}_t(\mu) &= \textstyle\frac12\prod_{s\le t}\bigl(1+\kappa\psi_s(\mu)\bigr)
                        + \frac12\prod_{s\le t}\bigl(1-\kappa\psi_s(\mu)\bigr),
  &\kappa &= c'/E,
\end{align*}
where $E$ is an a-priori envelope on $|\psi|$ and $c,c'<1$ keep every factor positive. The
first is the wealth of a bettor staking on the signal leading the outcome; the second is the
average of two bettors staking in opposite directions on $\mu$ being the wrong centre.

\begin{proposition}[Validity]
\label{prop:validity}
With $w\in(0,1)$, let
$\mathcal{E}_t=\inf_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl[w\,W^{\mathrm{sig}}_t(\mu)+(1-w)\,W^{\mathrm{ctr}}_t(\mu)\bigr]$.
Under $H_0$, $P\bigl(\exists t:\ \mathcal{E}_t\ge1/\alpha\bigr)\le\alpha$ in finite samples,
uniformly in $t$ and uniformly in the unknown drift.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
At the true drift $\mu^\star$ both factors are non-negative martingales with unit initial
value, hence so is their convex combination. An infimum over $\mu$ is bounded above by the
value at $\mu^\star$, so $\mathcal{E}$ is dominated by a non-negative martingale and Ville's
inequality applies. Predictability of $\lambda_s$ is what makes each factor fair; no choice of
$\lambda$ can affect validity, only power.
\end{proof}

The two terms play distinct roles. At a wrong $\mu$ the centring bettor gets rich, so the
infimum is not dragged to zero by candidates the data have already ruled out; at the true
$\mu$ the centring bettor makes nothing and the signal bettor drives the process. The nuisance
is \emph{eliminated} rather than estimated, and the elimination costs one line of proof rather
than a bootstrap. The infimum is a standard device for composite nulls and is not claimed as
new; it is also not growth-optimal, and the numeraire e-variable \citep{larsson2025} would be
tighter. Computing that for this null is the obvious next step and we have not done it.

\begin{remark}[The one numerical trap]
The infimum is computed on a grid, and the spacing must resolve the drift to better than its
sampling error $\hat\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. A grid spaced at $\hat\sigma$ places the numerical
infimum above the true one and leaks type-I error --- we measured $0.274$ at nominal $0.05$
before diagnosing it. Our released implementation exposes a resolution helper and a regression
test that reproduces the failure.
\end{remark}

\section{What it costs}

A construction that only reports its advantages is not useful for deciding whether to adopt
it. Table \ref{tab:power} compares power against Clark--West where Clark--West is
\emph{correctly} specified, so the comparison is the unfavourable one. At matched empirical
size the e-process needs roughly twice the sample to reach comparable power.

Part of that gap is the price of anytime-validity itself and is not specific to this
construction. Comparing the sample a test martingale needs to cross $1/\alpha$ with $80\%$
probability against the fixed-sample size for $80\%$ power at the same $\alpha$ gives a ratio
of $\mathbf{1.90}$. We state this because we previously stated it wrongly: an earlier draft
compared a \emph{median} crossing time with an $80\%$-power sample size and reported a $3\%$
cost. The corrected figure is a factor of two, and the correction is in the release history.

\section{A ceiling, not only a verdict}

Inverting the same construction over a value parameter rather than testing it against zero
gives a time-uniform confidence interval for $\theta=\mathbb{E}[z_t(y_t-\mu)]$, the expected
return of a position sized by the standardised signal. Its upper endpoint is a bound on
incremental predictive value that is available \emph{whether or not anything is rejected} ---
which is what a failed test cannot otherwise supply, since a non-rejection is consistent both
with no effect and with an effect the design could never have seen.

Applied to $117$ predictor--frequency pairs from a century of equity-premium data
\citep{goyal2024}, nothing is certified under any of nine specifications, and neither
Clark--West, the Clark--McCracken encompassing test, nor a model confidence set \citep{hansen2011} rejects on the
same forecasts. The interval is the informative output: across the $116$ unflagged pairs the
median marginal $95\%$ upper endpoint on the incremental annualised information ratio is
$0.60$, and $112$ exceed the $0.25$ that this literature treats as economically meaningful. A
century of the most studied data in finance separates neither hypothesis.

\section{Limitations}

The e-process is less powerful than Clark--West wherever Clark--West is well specified, and we
recommend it only where the drift is genuinely a nuisance, where monitoring is sequential, or
where a bound rather than a verdict is wanted. Validity requires an a-priori envelope on the
payoff, which must be asserted rather than fitted; a fitted envelope makes the stakes depend on
the future. The identity payoff assumes only a martingale-difference null, but bounded payoffs
such as $\tanh$ additionally require conditional symmetry, which we tested and rejected on our
own data (skew $-1.05$, $p=2.7\times10^{-78}$) --- hence identity is the default.
