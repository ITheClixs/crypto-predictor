\begin{abstract}
  Tests of out-of-sample return predictability dispose of the asset's drift by estimating it,
  and that is where they break. A model with an unpenalised intercept does not reduce to the
  zero forecast when its slopes vanish, so racing it against zero tests no drift \emph{and} no
  conditional predictability jointly. We show in closed form that the resulting Clark--West
  statistic converges to the $t$-statistic of the asset's mean return: at Bitcoin's drift a
  model with no features at all is called significantly predictive in $43\%$ of samples. We therefore eliminate the drift instead of estimating it. A \emph{certificate} is
  a non-negative wealth process---one bettor stakes on the signal, a second hedges every
  candidate drift---whose infimum over the nuisance is bounded by a martingale under the null. Validity is finite-sample and uniform in time, with no bandwidth, long-run
  variance, bootstrap, asymptotics or refitting, for arbitrary black-box pipelines. It can
  therefore be monitored daily with no correction for looking, and e-values average under
  arbitrary dependence, so a research grid needs no joint bootstrap and an overlapping
  $h$-step forecast combines into exactly the staggered $h$-vintage portfolio. The
  construction closes the sample-size question---the median time to certify an annualised
  information ratio $\mathrm{IR}$ is $2\ln(1/\alpha)/\mathrm{IR}^2$ years, about
  $6/\mathrm{IR}^2$ at $5\%$---and we are equally explicit about the price, which the e-value
  literature does not report for this comparison: matched on level and power the guarantee
  costs about twice the sample of a fixed-sample test, and against a correctly sized
  Clark--West we measure power of $0.48$ against $0.85$ at $\mathrm{IR}=2$. As a positive
  control the same instrument certifies volatility persistence on the same assets within
  $148$--$416$ days. On returns it certifies nothing in six years, and its ceiling bounds the
  incremental information ratio at $2.6$ without separating it from zero.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A forecasting pipeline produces out-of-sample predictions of an asset's return, and the
analyst wants to know one thing: do the features carry information, or has the model
rediscovered the fact that the asset went up? The question sounds like the one the standard
nested predictive-accuracy tests answer. It is not.

Those tests treat the unconditional drift as a nuisance parameter and dispose of it by
plugging in an estimate---either zero, or the recursively estimated sample mean. Plugging in
zero is worse than it looks. A regression fitted with an unpenalised intercept does not
reduce to the zero forecast when its slopes are set to zero; it reduces to the
training-window mean. Racing it against zero therefore tests \emph{no drift and no
conditional predictability} jointly, while the sentence such a test is used to support is
about the features alone. \citet{clark2006} are explicit that their null is a zero-mean
martingale difference, so the statistic is behaving exactly as specified; the error is in
the translation, and it is invisible in code. Section~\ref{sec:cw} makes the size of that
error exact: to first order the zero-benchmark Clark--West statistic \emph{is} the
$t$-statistic of the asset's mean return, and on an asset with Bitcoin's drift a model with
no features at all is declared significantly predictive in about two samples in five.
Plugging in the recursive mean removes that leading term and leaves a smaller one, the
covariance between the benchmark's own estimation error and the model's signal, which is
what the residual size distortion at longer horizons consists of.

The response of this paper is not a better estimate of the drift. It is to stop estimating
it.

Figure~\ref{fig:certificate} is the paper in three panels: what the drift does to the
standard test, what the new instrument says about this study, and what six years of daily
data can settle at all.

\paragraph{The instrument.} Section~\ref{sec:certificate} constructs a \emph{certificate}: a
non-negative process $\mathcal{E}_t$ with $\mathcal{E}_0=1$ whose expectation never exceeds
one under the composite null ``the features add nothing, at any drift''. Two bettors are run
against each other for every candidate drift---one staking on the signal leading the
outcome, one hedging the drift two-sidedly---and the certificate is the infimum over the
nuisance of their average. Because an infimum is at most the value at the truth, and the
value at the truth is a martingale, Ville's inequality gives
$P(\exists t:\mathcal{E}_t\ge1/\alpha)\le\alpha$ in finite samples and uniformly in time.
The proof is six lines and uses no asymptotics, no mixing condition, no long-run variance,
no bandwidth and no bootstrap. It is indifferent to what produced the forecasts, so
regularised, adaptively stopped and black-box learners are covered by the same statement
that covers ordinary least squares---which matters, because the asymptotic theory the
applied literature relies on was derived for none of them.

\paragraph{Three consequences that are not available to a $t$-statistic.} First, the
guarantee holds at every stopping time, so the process can be run forward on a live strategy
and inspected daily with no correction for looking, and it supplies an anytime-valid
\emph{ceiling} on incremental value---the number that lets a desk retire a research line
rather than merely fail to confirm it. Second, its logarithm is the profit and loss of an
explicit drift-hedged strategy, so statistical and economic significance are the same
quantity and rejecting at $5\%$ means twentyfold growth of capital that was credited with no
part of the market's return. Third, e-values average under arbitrary dependence, so an
eighteen-setting research grid is corrected by an average and an $h$-step overlapping
forecast by splitting capital across its $h$ daily vintages---which is exactly the staggered
portfolio the economic analysis needs, so the statistically valid combination and the
implementable strategy coincide.

\paragraph{The law, and its price.} Evidence is log wealth and log wealth grows at half the
squared information ratio, so the sample-size question closes in form: the median time to
certify an annualised information ratio $\mathrm{IR}$ is $2\ln(1/\alpha)/\mathrm{IR}^2$ years,
about $6/\mathrm{IR}^2$ at the $5\%$ level, and no anytime-valid test does better in order.
Six years of daily data---about what any liquid cryptocurrency offers---cannot certify an
information ratio below roughly $1.6$.

We are equally explicit about what the guarantee costs, because that is the number a
practitioner has to weigh and it is missing from the e-value literature for this comparison.
Matched on level \emph{and} power, the certificate needs about \emph{twice} the sample of a
one-sided fixed-sample test (Corollary~\ref{cor:free}), a ratio that is scale-free and sits
between $1.9$ and $2.2$ across conventional power levels. Against Clark--West itself, in the
design where Clark--West is correctly sized, Section~\ref{sec:headtohead} measures the loss
directly: at an annualised information ratio of $2$ the incumbent has power $0.85$ and the
certificate $0.31$. That is the exchange rate. Nothing in this paper claims the certificate
dominates; the claim is that it buys validity under weaker assumptions, at every stopping
time, across a dependent grid and for pipelines no asymptotic theory covers, and that the
price is roughly a doubling of the data.

\paragraph{The evidence.} Section~\ref{sec:results-tests} applies all of this to a
deliberately careful study: three assets, two horizons, six forecasters, purged and
embargoed walk-forward validation over 2020--2026, programmatic leakage tests, transaction
costs, execution delay and exposure regressions. Clark--West against zero rejects in five of
eighteen settings and against the recursive mean in seven, and a joint resampling of the
whole eighteen-test experiment puts $P(N\ge7)$ at well under one per cent---so under
$p$-values there is something to explain. Under certificates there is not: no setting is
certified, the grid-level e-value is $1.18$ against the $20$ required, the directional
variant that replaces an invalid market-timing test certifies nothing either, and the
ceilings bound the features' incremental annualised information ratio between $0.6$ and
$2.6$, every interval containing zero. The two readings are not in conflict. The design law says a six-year sample
cannot resolve ratios of this size, so the correct conclusion is that the question has not
been answered rather than that it has been answered negatively---and that distinction is
exactly what the ceiling, and not a $p$-value, is able to express.

\paragraph{What is new here, and what is not.}
The diagnosis in Section~\ref{sec:cw} is not a discovery and we state its provenance before
reporting it. The zero-mean null is Clark and West's own statement of their test
\citep{clark2006}; the recursively estimated mean has been the standard out-of-sample
benchmark in the equity-premium literature since \citet{welch2008} and \citet{campbell2008};
\citet{moosa2016} argue the drift-versus-no-drift choice directly; \citet{pincheira2021}
document Clark--West's long-horizon size distortion and propose the Wild Clark--West
replacement, which \citet{magner2022} apply to thirteen cryptocurrencies against both
benchmarks. What is new in Section~\ref{sec:cw} is only the closed form for the distortion
and its numerical validation.

The game-theoretic apparatus is likewise not ours. Test martingales, e-values and Ville's
inequality are the subject of an established literature
\citep{shafer2019, ramdas2023, waudbysmith2024}; \citet{wang2022} prove the e-BH result we
use; and \citet{choe2021} build confidence sequences for the difference in score between
\emph{two given} forecasters. What that literature does not contain, and what
Sections~\ref{sec:certificate}--\ref{sec:design} contribute, is the nested case: a
comparison in which the benchmark is not given but estimated from the same data, whose
nuisance is a drift shared by both forecasts, and whose null is therefore composite. The
specific new objects are the drift-eliminating construction of \eqref{eq:cert} and its
validity theorem, the concavity result that reduces the nuisance search to two evaluations
per step, the identification of the phase-averaged certificate with the staggered portfolio,
and the detection-horizon law with its optimality, anytime-validity and stake-learning
corollaries. The instrument is released as a library so that the claim can be checked on
someone else's pipeline rather than only on ours.

\paragraph{The four errors this study had to survive first.} Claims of accurate
cryptocurrency prediction are abundant and rarely survive scrutiny; the reported accuracy is
usually traceable to target leakage \citep{kaufman2012, kapoor2023}, preprocessing leakage,
an absent or misspecified benchmark \citep{timmermann2004}, or silent multiple testing
\citep{harvey2016}. Preprocessing leakage is impossible by construction here, because
standardisation is a pipeline step fit inside each fold; target leakage is prevented by a
test that perturbs future bars and asserts that no feature at or before $t$ changes;
multiple testing is handled by Theorem~\ref{thm:merge}. Benchmarking is the item this study
initially got wrong, and Appendix~\ref{app:audit} records what was claimed, why it was
wrong, and what replaced it.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Return predictability and out-of-sample evaluation.}
\citet{welch2008} showed that a long list of equity-premium predictors that
perform well in sample fail to beat a simple historical mean out of sample, and
\citet{campbell2008} formalised the out-of-sample $R^2$ against that benchmark
which we adopt in Section~\ref{sec:r2}. The lesson generalises: in-sample fit is
close to uninformative about forecast value, and the appropriate benchmark is a
naive one estimated recursively rather than with hindsight.

\paragraph{Predictive-accuracy tests for nested models.}
\citet{clark2001} give the encompassing tests for nested forecast comparison and
their non-standard limiting distributions; \citet{clark2006} specialise the
argument to the martingale-difference null, where the benchmark is the no-change
forecast and the null asserts a \emph{zero-mean} martingale difference;
\citet{clark2007} extend it to general nested pairs and report that with standard
normal critical values the actual size is close to, and usually a little below,
nominal. The degeneracy that makes the limit non-standard---under the null the two
models coincide, so the core statistic has zero variance at population
parameters---is diagnosed in \citet{west1996}. \citet{pincheira2021} remove it by
multiplying the larger model's error by an independent random variable with unit
mean and small variance, giving the asymptotically normal Wild Clark--West
statistic, which their simulations show is better sized at long horizons at some
cost in power. \citet{moosa2016} argue that whether the benchmark random walk
carries a drift is an empirical question rather than a settled one, and that the
answer changes which model wins. We use all of this rather than extend it;
Section~\ref{sec:calibration} measures how the resulting procedures behave in one
concrete pipeline.

\paragraph{Machine learning in asset pricing.}
\citet{gu2020} conduct a large-scale comparison of machine-learning methods for
equity returns and find measurable but small out-of-sample gains, concentrated in
methods that shrink aggressively. Our model roster is chosen in that spirit:
two regularised linear models and a shallow gradient booster (depth 3, learning
rate $0.03$, subsampling $0.8$, $\lambda = 1$, early stopping), on the premise
that an unregularised learner in this signal-to-noise regime fits noise. Those
hyperparameters were fixed by hand before the grid was run and never tuned, which
is a limitation and not a virtue: it keeps the search family small enough to
enumerate, but it means no conclusion here concerns a model \emph{family}.

\paragraph{Backtest overfitting.}
\citet{bailey2014pseudo} and \citet{arnott2019} document how repeated backtesting
manufactures apparently significant strategies, and \citet{bailey2014} propose the
deflated Sharpe ratio, which raises the bar in proportion to the number of trials.
\citet{white2000} and \citet{sullivan1999} address the same problem for technical
trading rules through a bootstrap reality check. We adopt the deflated Sharpe
ratio together with explicit control of the false discovery rate and the
family-wise error rate over our grid.

\paragraph{Cryptocurrency market efficiency.}
The empirical record is mixed and appears to be time-varying.
\citet{urquhart2016} reports evidence of inefficiency in early Bitcoin returns;
\citet{nadarajah2017} find that a simple power transformation of the same returns
satisfies efficiency tests, and \citet{tiwari2018} report that Bitcoin is largely
efficient over most of their sample. Closest to our setting,
\citet{magner2022} test 13 cryptocurrencies over 2018--2022 with autoregressive
models and lagged Bitcoin returns, evaluated by Wild Clark--West against both the
driftless random walk (a zero forecast) and the random walk with drift (a constant
forecast), and report that their models beat the benchmark in a majority of
exercises; \citet{pincheira2022} propose a further out-of-sample test aimed
specifically at the random-walk benchmark. Reporting both benchmarks is therefore
established practice in this literature, which is one reason we treat the
benchmark point below as a measurement rather than a discovery. Much of this work
evaluates statistical efficiency without asking whether any detected dependence
survives transaction costs, which we do take up.

\paragraph{Game-theoretic statistics.}
The instrument of Section~\ref{sec:certificate} is built from an established apparatus and it
is worth being precise about which parts are borrowed. Test martingales, e-values and Ville's
inequality \citep{ville1939} are developed in \citet{shafer2019} and surveyed by
\citet{ramdas2023}; the betting construction and its predictable stakes follow
\citet{waudbysmith2024}; \citet{vovk2021} establish the merging rules for e-values and
\citet{wang2022} the e-BH procedure we use for false-discovery control under arbitrary
dependence. \citet{kelly1956} and \citet{cover1991} supply the growth-rate arguments behind
Section~\ref{sec:design}. Closest in intent, \citet{choe2021} construct confidence sequences
for the difference in score between \emph{two given} forecasters, and \citet{henzi2022} give
sequentially valid inference on probability-forecast performance.

What that literature does not address is the case this paper needs. In a nested predictive
comparison the benchmark is not given; it is estimated from the same data, its estimation
error enters the statistic, and the nuisance it introduces---an unknown drift---is shared by
both forecasts, so the null is composite rather than simple. That is precisely the structure
Clark--West exists to handle, and it is the structure the game-theoretic literature has not
taken up. Equation~\eqref{eq:cert} and Theorem~\ref{thm:validity} are our answer to it, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:concave}, Remark~\ref{rem:stagger} and Theorem~\ref{thm:horizon} are
consequences specific to that setting. On the econometric side, \citet{giacomini2006} give an
alternative route to valid nested inference through a fixed rolling window; it is a
fixed-sample test, requires a non-vanishing estimation window, and does not remove the drift.

\paragraph{Validation for dependent data.}
Purged and embargoed cross-validation is due to \citet{lopezdeprado2018}, and is
the mechanism by which we prevent a training label whose realisation overlaps a
test window from informing the fit.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

We use daily OHLCV bars from Yahoo Finance for the USD pairs of BTC, ETH, and SOL. BTC and
ETH span 2019-01-01 to 2026-07-18 (2{,}756 bars each); SOL begins 2020-04-10 (2{,}291 bars).
Cryptocurrency markets trade every calendar day, so the bar index is contiguous with no
weekend or holiday gaps. We verified this rather than assuming it, and Section~\ref{sec:cv}
shows that the split logic remains conservative under gaps regardless.

\paragraph{Provenance, and what this source is and is not.}
Because the economic analysis of Section~\ref{sec:economics} turns on execution timing, the
construction of a daily bar is not a detail, and we state what we know and what we do not.
The \texttt{BTC-USD}, \texttt{ETH-USD} and \texttt{SOL-USD} series are Yahoo Finance's
\emph{composite} quotes aggregated across multiple venues, not the tape of any single
exchange. Bars are stamped in UTC and close at 00:00 UTC. Volume is summed across the
contributing venues. ``Adjusted close'' carries no meaning for a spot cryptocurrency pair---
there are no splits or dividends---and equals the close; we use the unadjusted close
throughout to avoid implying otherwise. Yahoo may revise historical bars without notice,
which is why every number in this paper is computed from a cache pinned at 2026-07-18 and
committed to the repository, with per-file SHA-256 digests recorded there; the configuration
pins the sample end date rather than resolving it to the current day, after an earlier
revision silently re-dated an entire Monte Carlo experiment.

Three consequences follow and none is favourable. A composite high or low need not be
executable on any single venue, so any strategy touching intrabar extremes would be
mis-priced---ours touches only closes, which is one reason it does. A composite close is not
a price at which an order can be filled, so the one-bar entry delay of
Section~\ref{sec:economics} is a necessary correction and not a sufficient one. And a study
that wanted to make a positive claim about tradeability, rather than the negative claim we
make, would need exchange-specific data from a venue on which the strategy could actually
have traded, with the order book and funding rates that go with it.

The evaluated out-of-sample window is shorter than the data window because the
first fold consumes 504 bars of training history. Table~\ref{tab:data} gives the
resulting per-setting counts.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Features}

Let $C_t, H_t, L_t, V_t$ denote close, high, low, and volume at bar $t$. Every
feature is a function of information available at or before $t$, and all twelve
are scale-free so that a single model can pool across assets trading at very
different price levels. We use cumulative log returns over
$k \in \{1,5,10,21\}$, realised volatility over $w \in \{10,21\}$, Wilder's RSI
over 14 bars recentred to roughly $[-1,1]$, a price-normalised MACD histogram,
two moving-average ratios, a mean normalised range, and a volume $z$-score:
%
\begin{align}
  r_t^{(k)} &= \log \frac{C_t}{C_{t-k}},
  &
  \sigma_t^{(w)} &= \operatorname{sd}\!\left(r_{t-w+1}^{(1)}, \dots, r_t^{(1)}\right),
  \\[2pt]
  \mathrm{RSI}_t &= 100 - \frac{100}{1 + \bar{U}_t / \bar{D}_t},
  &
  \mathrm{macd}_t &= \frac{M_t - S_t}{C_t},
  \\[2pt]
  \mathrm{ma}_t &= \log \frac{\mathrm{SMA}_7(C)_t}{\mathrm{SMA}_{21}(C)_t},
  &
  \mathrm{dist}_t &= \log \frac{C_t}{\mathrm{SMA}_{50}(C)_t},
  \\[2pt]
  \mathrm{range}_t &= \frac{1}{14}\sum_{i=0}^{13} \frac{H_{t-i} - L_{t-i}}{C_{t-i}},
  &
  z_t &= \frac{\log(1+V_t) - \mu_t^{(21)}}{s_t^{(21)}},
\end{align}
%
where $\bar U$ and $\bar D$ are exponentially smoothed gains and losses with
$\alpha = 1/14$, and $M_t = \mathrm{EMA}_{12}(C)_t - \mathrm{EMA}_{26}(C)_t$ with
$S_t = \mathrm{EMA}_9(M)_t$. The longest warm-up is 50 bars; rows with any
undefined feature are dropped.

The absence of look-ahead is enforced by a test rather than by inspection: future
bars are perturbed and every feature value at or before $t$ is asserted unchanged.
This catches an accidental centred window, a sign error in a shift, or a
non-causal smoother, none of which is reliably visible when reading code.

\subsection{Prediction target}

The label attached to a decision made at $t$ is the return realised over
$(t, t+h]$:
%
\begin{equation}
  y_t^{(h)} = \log \frac{C_{t+h}}{C_t}.
\end{equation}
%
The final $h$ rows have no label. We retain them with features and a missing
target, because that is exactly the state a live forecaster occupies on the most
recent bar, and we exclude them from fitting and scoring.

\subsection{Purged, embargoed walk-forward validation}
\label{sec:cv}

Ordinary $k$-fold cross-validation shuffles time and trains on the future. A plain
chronological split is also insufficient: because $y_t^{(h)}$ resolves at $t+h$, a
training row close to the test block carries a label whose realisation overlaps
the test window. Following \citet{lopezdeprado2018}, we purge the $h$ rows before
each test block and impose a further embargo of $e$ bars. Writing $\mathcal{T}_k$
and $\mathcal{V}_k$ for the train and test index sets of fold $k$, every fold
satisfies
%
\begin{equation}
  \max(\mathcal{T}_k) + h + e < \min(\mathcal{V}_k).
  \label{eq:purge}
\end{equation}
%
Test blocks tile forward without overlap. We use an expanding window with
$|\mathcal{T}| \ge 504$ bars, $|\mathcal{V}| = 63$ bars, and $e = 5$, giving 35
folds for BTC and ETH and 28 for SOL. Figure~\ref{fig:design} shows the resulting
layout, drawn from the splits the study actually runs.

\paragraph{The benchmark is refit on the same schedule as the model, not daily.}
Every forecaster, benchmark included, is estimated once per 63-bar test block, so the
intercept-only forecast is constant within a block and steps at block boundaries. This is
the exact slopes-zero restriction of a model trained once at the start of the block, which is
what makes the nesting claim of Section~\ref{sec:cw} literally true here. It is \emph{not}
the fully recursive historical mean of \citet{welch2008}, which is re-estimated at every
forecast origin. We call it the \emph{fold-level expanding mean} throughout, and readers
comparing to the equity-premium literature should keep the distinction in view: the two
coincide asymptotically and differ by at most one block of data at any origin.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig1_design.pdf}
  \caption{The validation design. Panel (a) shows all 35 folds on one timeline;
  panel (b) zooms on a single train/test boundary, where the purge and the embargo
  are individually visible. Equation~\eqref{eq:purge} is what the gap enforces.}
  \label{fig:design}
\end{figure}

Purging is applied by row position, whereas the leak it prevents is a calendar
one. Position-based purging remains valid under missing bars, and conservatively
so: gaps only stretch the timeline, so $h$ positions always span at least $h$
calendar days. We assert this on a synthetic index with a quarter of its bars
removed.

Two further controls are worth naming because they are commonly missed.
Standardisation is a pipeline step fit inside each fold, so it never sees test
rows. And the gradient booster purges its own early-stopping holdout: without a
gap, the last $h$ rows of its fitting slice carry labels realised inside that
holdout, so early stopping would be tuned on partly observed outcomes. We apply
the same $h$-bar purge one level down, and test it by corrupting exactly those
rows and asserting the fitted model is unchanged.

\subsection{Forecasters}

Three benchmarks and three machine-learning models are refit from scratch on every
fold. The benchmarks are the random walk $\hat{y}_t = 0$, the training-window mean
$\hat{y}_t = \bar{y}_{\mathcal{T}}$, and an AR(1) regression of $y^{(h)}$ on
$r^{(1)}$. The machine-learning models are ridge ($\alpha = 1$), elastic net
($\alpha = 10^{-3}$, $\rho = 0.5$), and XGBoost \citep{chen2016} with depth 3,
learning rate $0.03$, subsampling $0.8$, $\lambda = 1$, and early stopping on the
purged temporal holdout described above. The linear objectives are
%
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\beta}_{\text{ridge}} = \arg\min_{\beta} \lVert y - X\beta \rVert_2^2
  + \alpha \lVert \beta \rVert_2^2,
  \qquad
  \hat{\beta}_{\text{EN}} = \arg\min_{\beta} \frac{1}{2n}\lVert y - X\beta \rVert_2^2
  + \alpha \rho \lVert \beta \rVert_1 + \frac{\alpha(1-\rho)}{2}\lVert \beta \rVert_2^2 .
\end{equation}
%

Both linear objectives are fitted with an unpenalised intercept on standardised
features, and the gradient booster estimates its own base score from the training
labels. Setting the slopes to zero therefore returns the training-window mean of
the target, not zero: measured on BTC at $h=1$, the ridge and elastic-net
intercepts equal $\bar y_{\mathcal{T}}$ to machine precision. The forecaster these
models nest is the training-window mean, and the zero-return benchmark is the
further restriction that the intercept is also zero. Section~\ref{sec:cw} shows
what that distinction does to the test.

\subsection{Trading rule and transaction costs}
\label{sec:strategy}

Forecasts become unit positions on a non-overlapping schedule: one decision every
$h$ bars, held to the next. Sampling every $h$-th bar prevents the same $h$-day
return being counted $h$ times. Costs are charged on turnover, so reversing a
position pays for two sides:
%
\begin{equation}
  w_t = \operatorname{sign}(\hat{y}_t) \in \{-1, 0, +1\},
  \qquad
  \tau_t = |w_t - w_{t-1}|,
  \qquad
  \pi_t = w_t\!\left(e^{y_t^{(h)}} - 1\right) - \tau_t c,
\end{equation}
%
with $c = 17$ basis points per side (10 bp taker fee, 5 bp slippage, 2 bp
half-spread). Equity compounds as $E_T = \prod_{t \le T}(1 + \pi_t)$ from unity.

Because decisions are taken every $h$ bars, the Sharpe ratio annualises at $365/h$
rather than at 365. Using the latter inflates a seven-day strategy's Sharpe by a
factor of $\sqrt{7} \approx 2.6$.

The schedule admits $h$ start offsets and nothing distinguishes them. We therefore
run every strategy on all $h$ and report the spread; at $h=7$ it reaches 1.40
Sharpe points on one setting (Section~\ref{sec:phase}).

An always-long reference is computed on the same schedule with the same cost
model, so that a forecaster whose only achievement is holding the asset can be
recognised as such.

\subsection{Out-of-sample $\rsq$}
\label{sec:r2}

We report the Campbell--Thompson form \citep{campbell2008}, whose denominator is
the squared error of a genuine ex-ante benchmark forecast rather than of the
realised mean of the evaluation window:
%
\begin{equation}
  \rsq = 1 - \frac{\sum_t (y_t - \hat{y}_t)^2}{\sum_t (y_t - \hat{y}^{\,b}_t)^2},
\end{equation}
%
with $\hat{y}^{\,b}$ the recursively estimated historical mean. That benchmark
scores exactly zero against itself, which serves as a wiring check on the metric.
The alternative denominator $\sum_t (y_t - \bar{y})^2$ uses a quantity unknowable
in advance and flatters the benchmark; we compute it as a cross-check but do not
quote it.

Note that this is a \emph{different} benchmark from the one the predictive-accuracy
tests of Sections~\ref{sec:hac} and~\ref{sec:cw} use, and reporting effect sizes
against one reference while testing significance against another is not defensible.
It also happens to be the easier reference here: the zero forecast has lower squared
error than the recursive mean in all six asset--horizon cells, by $\rsq$ of $+0.0005$
(BTC $h{=}1$) up to $+0.1023$ (SOL $h{=}7$), the recursive drift estimate on SOL being
badly wrong after the early-sample run-up. Measured against the zero forecast instead,
exactly one of 18 settings has positive $\rsq$: BTC elastic net at $h=1$, at
$+0.0026$. Table~\ref{tab:accuracy} quotes the recursive-mean column; the
zero-forecast comparison is the stricter one and is the number to read alongside a
$p$-value computed against the zero forecast.

\subsection{Diebold--Mariano and the nested-model problem}
\label{sec:hac}

For loss differential $d_t = L(e^m_t) - L(e^b_t)$ under squared-error loss, the
Diebold--Mariano statistic \citep{diebold1995} is $\mathrm{DM} = \bar{d}/\sqrt{\hat{V}/n}$
with $\hat V$ a Newey--West long-run variance \citep{newey1987} truncated at $h-1$
Bartlett lags, appropriate because $h$-step forecast errors follow an MA($h-1$):
%
\begin{equation}
  \hat{V} = \hat{\gamma}_0 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{h-1}\left(1 - \frac{k}{h}\right)\hat{\gamma}_k,
  \qquad
  \hat{\gamma}_k = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=k+1}^{n}(d_t - \bar{d})(d_{t-k} - \bar{d}).
\end{equation}
%
The $1/n$ normalisation applies to every autocovariance including the lagged ones.
This is deliberate: the Bartlett weights guarantee $\hat V \ge 0$ only under that
normalisation, and the more intuitive $1/(n-k)$ can drive a weighted sum negative
in small samples. We apply the Harvey--Leybourne--Newbold small-sample correction
\citep{harvey1997},
$\mathrm{DM}^{\ast} = \mathrm{DM}\sqrt{(n + 1 - 2h + h(h-1)/n)/n}$, against a
$t_{n-1}$ reference. A negative statistic favours the model, and the test is
two-sided because the null is equal accuracy and a model can be significantly
worse.

The MA($h-1$) argument justifies the bandwidth for the \emph{forecast error} under
the null. It does not carry over to $\hat f_t$ once Equation~\eqref{eq:drift}
applies, because the drift component $2\mathbb{E}[y](\hat y^{\,m}_t - \hat
y^{\,b}_t)$ inherits the persistence of the forecast. At $h=1$ the bandwidth is
zero lags, so no autocovariance enters at all, and four of the five settings that
reject in Section~\ref{sec:results-tests} are at $h=1$. We therefore report both
$h-1$ and the Newey--West plug-in bandwidth $\lfloor 4(n/100)^{2/9}\rfloor$, which
is 8 lags at $n \approx 2{,}200$. On this data the plug-in bandwidth yields
\emph{smaller} $p$-values---the adjusted differentials are mildly negatively
autocorrelated, so the $h-1$ choice is the conservative one at $h=1$---but the count
of surviving settings changes with it. The two bandwidths turn out to be
interchangeable as far as size goes: under the independent null of
Section~\ref{sec:calibration} they differ by at most $0.3$ percentage points in
every cell we measured. What separates them is the rejection count on the real data,
and nothing in the data selects between them, so we report the sensitivity as a
result rather than choosing.

\subsection{Clark--West}
\label{sec:cw}

Under a nested null, Diebold--Mariano is undersized as a test of predictability:
the larger model pays sample mean squared prediction error to estimate
coefficients that are truly zero, and the test reads that cost as evidence for the
benchmark. \citet{clark2007} subtract the term explicitly. With $\hat{y}^{\,b}$
the nested benchmark and $\hat{y}^{\,m}$ the larger model,
%
\begin{equation}
  \hat{f}_t = \left(y_t - \hat{y}^{\,b}_t\right)^2
  - \left[\left(y_t - \hat{y}^{\,m}_t\right)^2
  - \left(\hat{y}^{\,b}_t - \hat{y}^{\,m}_t\right)^2\right]
  = 2\left(y_t - \hat{y}^{\,b}_t\right)\left(\hat{y}^{\,m}_t - \hat{y}^{\,b}_t\right),
  \label{eq:cw}
\end{equation}
%
and $\bar{f}/\sqrt{\hat{V}_f/n}$ is referred to a standard normal, one-sided. A
\emph{positive} statistic favours the model, the opposite of the Diebold--Mariano
convention; we report both statistics with their $p$-values throughout so the sign
is never inferred from significance alone.

\paragraph{What the benchmark choice does to the hypothesis.}
The right-hand identity in Equation~\eqref{eq:cw} is the whole issue. Clark--West
is a $t$-test of whether the model's \emph{deviation from the benchmark} covaries
positively with the outcome's deviation from it. With $\hat y^{\,b} = 0$ the
identity collapses to $\hat f_t = 2 y_t \hat y^{\,m}_t$, whose expectation is
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}\!\left[\hat f_t\right] = 2\left(\mathbb{E}[y]\,\mathbb{E}[\hat y^{\,m}]
  + \mathrm{Cov}\!\left(y, \hat y^{\,m}\right)\right).
  \label{eq:drift}
\end{equation}
%
Only the second term is predictability. The first is drift multiplied by the
average forecast, and it is nonzero whenever the sample has a drift and the model
is on average long---which, through the fitted intercept, it always is here. BTC's
realised drift over this sample is $+0.00102$ per day, 37\% annualised. Measured
across the 18 settings, that term accounts for 9\% of the numerator at BTC $h=1$
but 33\% for the booster at $h=1$ and 41--61\% across BTC $h=7$. The benchmark
forecasters make the point most directly: the training-window mean, which uses no
features at all, scores $\mathrm{CW} = +1.03$ against the zero forecast on BTC at
$h=1$.

\paragraph{How large the contamination is.} Equation~\eqref{eq:drift} says the drift term is
present; the following says how big it gets, and it is bigger than the qualitative statement
suggests.

\begin{proposition}[The zero-benchmark statistic is a drift test]
\label{prop:distortion}
Let $y_t$ be i.i.d.\ with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Let the larger model be the
intercept-only forecast $\hat\mu_t$ estimated on a window of $k$ observations disjoint from
$t$, plus the out-of-sample contribution of $p$ exogenous features whose coefficients are
truly zero, so that $\hat y^{\,m}_t=\hat\mu_t+u_t$ with $\mathbb{E}[u_t]=0$ and
$\operatorname{Var}(u_t)\approx p\sigma^2/k$. The correct answer is always ``no incremental
predictability''. Then the Clark--West adjusted differential against the zero benchmark,
$\hat f_t = 2y_t\hat y^{\,m}_t$, has
\[
  \mathbb{E}[\hat f_t] = 2\mu^2,\qquad
  \operatorname{Var}(\hat f_t) \approx 4\Big(\mu^2\sigma^2+\sigma^4\tfrac{1+p}{k}\Big),
\]
so the statistic computed on $n$ test points satisfies
\[
  \mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{CW}_0\big]\;\approx\;
  \frac{\sqrt{n}\,S^{2}}{\sqrt{S^{2}+(1+p)/k}},\qquad S=\mu/\sigma .
\]
For any $\mu\neq0$ this diverges in $n$, so the rejection probability tends to one. Two
limits are worth naming. When $kS^2\gg 1+p$ it reduces to $\sqrt{n}\,S$: \emph{the statistic
becomes the $t$-statistic of the asset's mean return}. When $kS^2\ll 1+p$ it is
$\sqrt{nk/(1+p)}\,S^2$, so a model that estimates more parameters dilutes the drift signal
with its own estimation noise and the distortion shrinks---which is why the study's
regularised twelve-feature models show a smaller distortion than a featureless one.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\hat y^{\,m}_t$ is independent of $y_t$ with mean $\mu$ and variance
$\sigma^2(1+p)/k$, so $\mathbb{E}[2y_t\hat y^{\,m}_t]=2\mu^2$ and
$\operatorname{Var}(2y_t\hat y^{\,m}_t)=
4[\operatorname{Var}(y)\mathbb{E}[\hat y^{\,m}]^2+
\operatorname{Var}(\hat y^{\,m})\mathbb{E}[y]^2+
\operatorname{Var}(y)\operatorname{Var}(\hat y^{\,m})]$.
Substituting and dividing by $\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\hat f)/n}$ gives the display after
dropping the term of order $\mu^2/k$. \qed
\end{proof}

estimated coefficients that are truly zero dilutes the effect to $11.8\%$, which is the
regime this study's models occupy and is consistent with the $14$--$21\%$ measured by the
full-refit experiment of Section~\ref{sec:calibration}. Either way, nothing about the
features is being measured: the test is reading the asset's drift, and the applied reader is
told the features predict returns. The rate rises to $81\%$ at an annualised Sharpe ratio of
$2.0$, which several cryptocurrencies have printed over multi-year windows.

None of this is a fault in the statistic. \citet{clark2006} define the null they
test as a zero-mean martingale difference, so a drift term entering
Equation~\eqref{eq:drift} is the test doing what it says. The fault is in the
translation: an applied paper that races a feature model against zero and reports
``the features predict returns'' has silently added ``and the mean return is
zero'' to its null. We therefore report Clark--West against both the zero forecast,
because that is what much of the applied literature does, and against the
recursively estimated mean, which is the actual slopes-zero restriction of these
estimators and the benchmark already used for $\rsq$ in Section~\ref{sec:r2}.
Reporting one without the other conflates two hypotheses. \citet{magner2022} report
both for cryptocurrencies, and \citet{moosa2016} argue the general case.

\subsection{Calibrating the test instead of assuming it}
\label{sec:calibration}

Whether a rejection count is surprising depends on the test's size, which we
measure rather than assume. Resampling the daily log-return series into a synthetic
price path makes future returns independent of every past-information feature by
construction, so the null ``the features add no predictive value'' is true, while
the realised drift, the fat tails, the feature collinearity and persistence, the
sample length, the purged walk-forward geometry, and the estimators themselves are
all retained. Forecasts are genuinely estimated at every origin, so the
estimation-noise term Clark--West exists to remove is present---which is what
distinguishes this from a simulation in which the larger model's forecast is
exogenous noise. In that latter construction the population mean squared prediction
errors are $\sigma_y^2$ and $\sigma_y^2 + \sigma_u^2$, so the null of equal
accuracy is false rather than true, no coefficient is estimated, and Clark--West
centres for the algebraic reason that $\mathbb{E}[2 y_t u_t] = 0$ for any
independent $u$ however poor a forecast it is. Such a design measures neither
size nor bias, and an earlier version of this paper used it.

Resampling admits three nulls, and the distinction between them turns out to matter
more than any other choice in this section.

\begin{description}
  \item[Independent.] Returns are drawn iid. All dependence is destroyed, including
    the volatility clustering that daily crypto returns certainly have, so this
    measures size against a null cleaner than reality.
  \item[Block.] Returns are drawn in 21-day blocks. Volatility clustering survives,
    but so does whatever genuine within-block predictability the real series
    contains, so the rejection rate is an \emph{upper bound} on size rather than a
    measurement of it.
  \item[Sign-flipped.] Blocks are drawn as above, then the sign of each deviation
    from the sample mean is randomised: $r_t = \mu + s_t (r^{\ast}_t - \mu)$ with
    $s_t = \pm 1$ independent. Because $|r_t - \mu|$ is untouched bar for bar, the
    volatility dynamics survive intact; because $s_t$ is independent noise,
    $\mathbb{E}[r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = \mu$ exactly, so no conditional mean
    predictability remains. This is the null the other two cannot isolate: realistic
    dependence together with a true no-predictability hypothesis.
\end{description}

Four readings follow.

First, the configuration in standard use rejects three to six times as often as
nominal, and the drift-only row locates the cause: a forecaster with no feature
information at all, which merely predicts the training-window mean, is called
significantly better than the zero forecast in 27--42\% of samples that contain no
predictability whatever.

Second, substituting the recursive mean repairs it. Size is 3.1--3.7\% at $h=1$ and
5.7--5.9\% at $h=7$ under the independent null---slightly conservative, consistent
with \citet{clark2007}'s own report that the normal approximation is a little
undersized---and it does so for the early-stopped booster as readily as for ridge.
Greedy split selection, subsampling and a data-dependent number of trees do not
visibly break the approximation here. That is calibration evidence for this
estimator under this null, not a theorem, and we found no prior measurement of it.

Third, the dependence question has a cleaner answer than the block column suggests, but a
more careful one than an earlier version of this paper gave. Under block resampling the
corrected test rejects 30--45\% of the time at $h=1$, which that version read as a size
failure under dependence. Under the sign-flipped null, which preserves the same volatility
dynamics but removes conditional predictability by construction, size is 3.0--4.8\% at $h=1$
and 6.8--7.2\% at $h=7$. It is tempting to attribute the entire gap to genuine within-block
predictability that block resampling carries into the synthetic path, and that attribution is
not identified. Independent sign randomisation removes conditional mean dependence, but it
also removes other things, and Table~\ref{tab:nulls} measures which: skewness falls from
$-1.05$ to $-0.19$, the leverage correlation from $-0.071$ to $-0.003$, and the
autocorrelation of return \emph{signs} from $-0.045$ to $+0.001$. Any of those could move a
finite-sample statistic. The correct statement is that the gap is consistent with dependence
structures the block bootstrap retains and sign randomisation removes, of which conditional
mean dependence is one candidate among several.

What settles the question is not a sharper reading of two nulls but a third that shares
neither's artifacts. A GARCH(1,1) fitted to the same returns with the conditional mean held
at the sample drift generates volatility clustering parametrically (autocorrelation of
absolute returns $0.141$, against $0.154$ in the real series) while reproducing almost none of
the tail thickness (excess kurtosis $0.63$ against $18.8$), no leverage effect and no sign
dependence. It is wrong in different ways from the resampling nulls, which is exactly what
makes it useful. All three agree: Clark--West against the recursive mean rejects $5.25\%$,
$6.25\%$ and $5.00\%$ under block, sign-flipped and GARCH nulls respectively, against zero it
rejects $10.0\%$, $14.5\%$ and $9.0\%$, and the certificate rejects $0.25\%$, $1.50\%$ and
$0.25\%$ (Monte Carlo standard error $1.1$ points, $400$ replications each). The conclusion
does not depend on which fiction generates the data, which is the only form of reassurance a
resampling calibration can honestly offer.

\paragraph{What effect would this design detect?}
Size answers half the question; a study that reports a null owes the other half. We index the
alternative by an effect size rather than a nuisance scale: the sign-flipped deviations are
passed through an AR(1) filter,
$r_t - \mu = \rho\,(r_{t-1} - \mu) + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,d_t$, which holds the unconditional
variance fixed and gives the optimal one-step predictor a population $\rsq$ of exactly
$\rho^2$. The one-bar return is in the feature set, so the estimators can find it.

This number should be read narrowly. It is a \emph{scenario-specific} power curve, not the

are the two linear ones. Power against a nonlinear, regime-dependent, or unstable alternative,

conclusions survive that caveat, and they cut in opposite directions: this design is not
informative about effects below roughly one percent of return variance, so we make no claim

would consume it. Section~\ref{sec:design} gives the general version of this calculation,
which is not scenario-specific because it depends on the alternative only through its
information ratio.

\subsection{Sign timing}
\label{sec:sign}

A model can be poor at magnitude and useful at direction, which is all a sign
strategy requires. \citet{pesaran1992} test independence between the sign of the
forecast and the sign of the outcome; their statistic $S$ is asymptotically
standard normal, positive for sign-timing skill and negative for a reliably
wrong-way forecast. Appendix~\ref{sec:formulas} states it. The assumption that
matters here is that every variance term in it divides by $n$, so the $n$
observations must be independent. Consecutive $h$-step forecasts built from
overlapping windows are not, and Section~\ref{sec:results-signs} reports what that
did to our own numbers. The statistic is undefined when the forecast never changes
sign, which is the case for the always-long drift models, and we report it as
missing rather than as a number.

\subsection{Sharpe ratio, deflation, and interval estimates}

Sharpe ratios are noisy in short samples \citep{lo2002} and inflated by selection.
We report the probabilistic Sharpe ratio of \citet{bailey2014}, the probability
that the true Sharpe exceeds a threshold $\mathrm{SR}^{\ast}$ after correcting for
sample length, skewness and kurtosis, and the deflated Sharpe ratio, which sets
$\mathrm{SR}^{\ast}$ to the expected maximum over $N$ trials so that the bar rises
with the size of the search. Appendix~\ref{sec:formulas} states both. Here $N$ is
the number of models raced within one asset--horizon, which is a floor on the true
search and not an estimate of it, so the reported deflated Sharpe is an upper bound
and is labelled as such. Confidence intervals come from a circular block bootstrap
\citep{politis1994} with block length $\lfloor n^{1/3} \rceil$ and 500 resamples,
reported as percentile intervals.

\subsection{Multiple testing}

Eighteen settings tested by a correctly sized 5\% test would produce
$0.05 \times 18 = 0.9$ rejections from noise alone. That is the right calculation
only for a test with the size it claims. Averaging the sign-flipped column of
Table~\ref{tab:size} over the measured model--horizon combinations, the expected
count is about $4$ against the zero forecast and about $1$ against the recursive
mean. The zero-benchmark expectation is more than four times the nominal one, and
the recursive-mean expectation is close to it. These, not $0.9$, are the numbers a
raw count must be read against, and which of them applies depends entirely on the
benchmark. We report raw counts alongside two
adjustments of the Clark--West $p$-values over the family of 18. Holm--Bonferroni
\citep{holm1979} controls the family-wise error rate and Benjamini--Hochberg
\citep{benjamini1995} the false discovery rate:
%
\begin{equation}
  p^{\mathrm{Holm}}_{(i)} = \max_{j \le i}\, \min\!\left\{(m - j + 1)\,p_{(j)},\, 1\right\},
  \qquad
  p^{\mathrm{BH}}_{(i)} = \min_{j \ge i}\, \min\!\left\{\tfrac{m}{j}\,p_{(j)},\, 1\right\}.
\end{equation}
%
Benchmarks are excluded from the family: they are the null being tested against,
not candidates in the search.

\section{Certificates of incremental predictive ability}
\label{sec:certificate}

Section~\ref{sec:cw} located the problem in the benchmark and Section~\ref{sec:calibration}
measured what it costs. Neither repairs it. Replacing the zero forecast with the recursive
mean removes the leading drift term but leaves the residual
$-\mathbb{E}[(\hat\mu_t-\mu)\,g_t]$, the covariance between the benchmark's own estimation
error and the model's signal; that residual is what the $7$--$8\%$ measured size at $h=7$
in Table~\ref{tab:size} consists of. The deeper difficulty is structural. Every test in this
family handles the drift by \emph{estimating} it, and an estimated nuisance leaves an
estimation error inside the statistic.

This section eliminates the drift instead. The construction is a wealth process, and the
three properties that follow---finite-sample validity for the composite null, a growth rate
that equals the strategy's own profit and loss, and a merging rule that needs no dependence
assumption---are theorems rather than simulation results.

\subsection{Setup}

Let $(\mathcal{F}_t)_{t\ge0}$ be a filtration, $y_t$ the realised outcome at $t$ and
$g_t$ any $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$-measurable \emph{signal}. In a nested forecast comparison the
signal of interest is
%
\begin{equation}
  g_t \;=\; \hat y^{\,m}_t - \hat y^{\,b}_t,
  \label{eq:signal}
\end{equation}
%
the model's forecast minus the intercept-only forecast produced by the same estimation
window and refitting schedule, so that the hypothesis under test is exactly the one an
applied paper means: \emph{the features add nothing beyond the intercept}. Validity, as will
be seen, does not depend on this choice; only power does.

We test the composite null
%
\begin{equation}
  H_0 \;=\; \bigcup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}} P_\mu,
  \label{eq:null}
\end{equation}
%
where under $P_\mu$ the sequence $y_t-\mu$ is a martingale difference with respect to
$(\mathcal{F}_t)$. The union over $\mu$ is the point: the drift is a nuisance and is not
assumed, estimated or plugged in. Two variants of the test are available and they differ
only in what they ask of $P_\mu$.

\begin{description}
  \item[Assumption M (martingale).] $\mathbb{E}[y_t\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\mu$ and
    $|y_t|\le R$ almost surely for a constant $R$ fixed in advance.
  \item[Assumption S (symmetry).] $y_t-\mu$ is conditionally symmetric about zero given
    $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$.
\end{description}
%
Assumption~S has a property worth stating plainly because it is easy to adopt by accident:
conditional symmetry about a \emph{constant} $\mu$ implies unconditional symmetry, hence zero
skewness, so the assumption is refutable from the marginal distribution alone. On this
paper's data it is refuted---sample skewness $-1.05$, $-0.96$ and $-0.23$ on BTC, ETH and
SOL. The identity payoff, which needs only Assumption~M, is therefore the default throughout,
and the bounded payoffs are reported as sensitivity analyses rather than as the headline.
%
Assumption~S implies Assumption~M without the envelope. It is stronger than a martingale
difference and far weaker than independence: it permits arbitrary volatility clustering,
arbitrary tail thickness, and any dependence whatsoever in $|y_t-\mu|$. It is also exactly
the null the sign-flipped resampling of Section~\ref{sec:calibration} constructs, which is
why the two devices agree.

Fix an $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$-measurable scale $s_t>0$ and an odd payoff $\phi$. Under
Assumption~S any bounded odd $\phi$ satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\phi((y_t-\mu)/s_t)\mid
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=0$, because scaling by a positive predictable quantity preserves
conditional symmetry; we use $\phi=\tanh$ for magnitude and $\phi=\operatorname{sign}$ for
direction. Under Assumption~M only $\phi(u)=u$ is available, and the envelope $R$ enters
the stake ceiling below.

\subsection{Construction}

For a candidate drift $\mu$ write $\phi_t(\mu)=\phi\big((y_t-\mu)/s_t\big)$ and let
$\lambda_t\ge0$ and $\kappa_t>0$ be $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$-measurable stakes obeying
$\lambda_t|g_t|B_t\le c<1$ and $\kappa_tB_t\le c<1$, where $B_t$ is a predictable bound on
$|\phi_t|$ ($B_t=1$ for bounded $\phi$; $B_t=(R+\max|\mu|)/s_t$ for the identity payoff).
Define
%
\begin{align}
  W^{\mathrm{sig}}_t(\mu) &= \prod_{u\le t}\Big(1+\lambda_u\,g_u\,\phi_u(\mu)\Big),
  \label{eq:wsig}\\[2pt]
  W^{\mathrm{ctr}}_t(\mu) &= \tfrac12\prod_{u\le t}\Big(1+\kappa_u\phi_u(\mu)\Big)
                           + \tfrac12\prod_{u\le t}\Big(1-\kappa_u\phi_u(\mu)\Big),
  \label{eq:wctr}\\[2pt]
  \mathcal{E}_t &= \inf_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}\;
                   \tfrac12\Big(W^{\mathrm{sig}}_t(\mu)+W^{\mathrm{ctr}}_t(\mu)\Big).
  \label{eq:cert}
\end{align}
%
The two processes are two bettors. The first stakes on the signal genuinely leading the
outcome. The second stakes, two-sidedly, on $\mu$ being the wrong centre. At the true drift
the second bettor breaks even and the infimum is driven by the first; at a wrong drift the
second bettor gets rich, so candidates the data have ruled out cannot drag the infimum
down. The nuisance is hedged, not estimated.

\begin{theorem}[Validity]
\label{thm:validity}
Under Assumption~S (or Assumption~M with $\phi$ the identity), for every $P_{\mu_0}\in H_0$
and every $\alpha\in(0,1)$,
\[
  P_{\mu_0}\Big(\exists\,t\ge1:\;\mathcal{E}_t\ge 1/\alpha\Big)\;\le\;\alpha .
\]
Consequently $1/\sup_{t}\mathcal{E}_t$ is a $p$-value valid at every stopping time, and
$\mathcal{E}_\tau$ is an e-value for any stopping time $\tau$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $\mu=\mu_0$. Since $\phi$ is odd and $(y_u-\mu_0)/s_u$ is conditionally symmetric about
zero given $\mathcal{F}_{u-1}$, $\mathbb{E}[\phi_u(\mu_0)\mid\mathcal{F}_{u-1}]=0$; under
Assumption~M with $\phi(u)=u$ the same holds by the martingale property. The multipliers
$\lambda_ug_u$ and $\pm\kappa_u$ are $\mathcal{F}_{u-1}$-measurable, so each factor in
\eqref{eq:wsig} and \eqref{eq:wctr} has conditional expectation one and, by the stake
ceilings, is strictly positive. Each product is therefore a non-negative martingale started
at one; $W^{\mathrm{ctr}}$ is an average of two such, and the bracket in \eqref{eq:cert} is
an average of two, hence itself a non-negative martingale started at one. Finally
$\mathcal{E}_t\le\tfrac12(W^{\mathrm{sig}}_t(\mu_0)+W^{\mathrm{ctr}}_t(\mu_0))$ pointwise,
because an infimum is at most the value at $\mu_0$. Ville's inequality applied to that
martingale gives the bound. \qed
\end{proof}

Nothing in the proof uses asymptotics, mixing conditions, a long-run variance estimate, a
bandwidth, or a bootstrap. Nothing in it is disturbed by the estimator inside the pipeline
being regularised, adaptively stopped, or a black box: $g_t$ enters only through being
known one step ahead of the outcome. And nothing in it is disturbed by looking at
$\mathcal{E}_t$ every day, which is what makes the process usable as a live monitor.

\begin{lemma}[Concavity, and the fast path]
\label{lem:concave}
For $\phi(u)=u$, $\mu\mapsto\log W^{\mathrm{sig}}_t(\mu)$ is concave, so its infimum over
any interval is attained at an endpoint.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\partial^2_\mu\log\big(1+\lambda_ug_u(y_u-\mu)/s_u\big)
 = -\big(\lambda_ug_u/s_u\big)^2\big/\big(1+\lambda_ug_u(y_u-\mu)/s_u\big)^2\le 0$,
and a sum of concave functions is concave. \qed
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:concave} matters operationally rather than theoretically: with the identity
payoff the drift search costs two evaluations per step instead of a grid, so the certificate
runs in $O(n)$ time and $O(1)$ memory and can be updated online, one observation at a time.
For bounded odd payoffs concavity can fail and the infimum in \eqref{eq:cert} is evaluated
numerically; Section~\ref{sec:implementation} reports the grid refinement.

\subsection{The statistic is a profit and loss}

\begin{theorem}[Evidence in units of capital]
\label{thm:pnl}
Let $\phi$ be the identity payoff. Then
$\log\tfrac12\big(W^{\mathrm{sig}}_T(\mu)+W^{\mathrm{ctr}}_T(\mu)\big)$ is the cumulative
log return of a self-financing strategy that at each date holds $\lambda_tg_t/s_t$ units of
the asset, funded at rate $\mu$, and splits capital equally with a two-sided hedge of that
funding rate. Rejecting $H_0$ at level $\alpha$ requires that strategy to have multiplied its
capital by $1/\alpha$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[The interpretation is literal only for the identity payoff]
\label{rem:tradeable}
For a bounded odd $\phi$ such as $\tanh$ the same algebra holds, but the ``asset'' pays
$\phi\big((y_t-\mu)/s_t\big)$, and no such claim trades. The wealth process is then still a
valid e-process --- Theorem~\ref{thm:validity} does not care --- but it is the profit and loss
of a strategy in a synthetic derivative, not of one a desk could run. We flag this because the
identity between statistical and economic significance is the paper's most quotable claim and
it is exact in one case and figurative in the other. It is one reason the identity payoff is
the default.
\end{remark}

The theorem is immediate from \eqref{eq:wsig}--\eqref{eq:cert}---each factor is one plus a
period return---but its content is not. Statistical significance and economic significance
are usually separate quantities that a study reports side by side and hopes will agree; here
they are the same number, and the exchange rate is fixed. At $\alpha=0.05$ the requirement
is a twentyfold gain, $\log 20 = 3.00$ nats, earned by a strategy that is credited with no
part of the asset's drift. A model that is long the market and nothing else earns none of
it, by construction rather than by a regression on buy-and-hold run afterwards.

This also dissolves a question the two-metric literature keeps asking. When a study's
statistically significant settings and its economically profitable settings are disjoint,
the natural reading is that one metric is measuring something the other is not. Under
Theorem~\ref{thm:pnl} there is only one metric, and disjointness cannot arise.

\subsection{Multiplicity and overlap, without a bootstrap}
\label{sec:merge}

\begin{theorem}[Merging]
\label{thm:merge}
Let $E_1,\dots,E_m$ be e-values for the same null, and $w_i\ge0$ with $\sum_iw_i=1$. Then
$\sum_iw_iE_i$ is an e-value, \emph{whatever the dependence between the $E_i$}. If the
$E_i$ are e-values and $\alpha\in(0,1)$, the e-Benjamini--Hochberg procedure---reject the
$k$ largest for the largest $k$ with $E_{(k)}\ge m/(\alpha k)$---controls the false
discovery rate at $\alpha$ under arbitrary dependence \citep{wang2022}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[of the first claim]
$\mathbb{E}[\sum_iw_iE_i]=\sum_iw_i\mathbb{E}[E_i]\le\sum_iw_i=1$ by linearity, which uses
no joint distribution. \qed
\end{proof}

This is the whole of the multiple-testing apparatus this paper needs. The eighteen settings
share features, assets, horizons, market regimes and two nearly identical estimators; under
$p$-values that dependence forces either a conservative bound or a joint resampling
experiment, and Section~\ref{sec:joint} runs the latter for comparison at a cost of several
hours of computation. Under e-values the average of the eighteen certificates is already a
valid test of the global null, and no dependence needs to be modelled, estimated or
simulated.

\paragraph{Overlapping horizons.} An $h$-step forecast made every day produces overlapping
outcomes, so the per-day payoffs are not a martingale difference sequence and no wealth
process can be built on them directly. This is the defect that invalidates the averaged
Pesaran--Timmermann statistic of Section~\ref{sec:sign}: $h$ phase-specific standardised
statistics were averaged and the average referred to a standard normal, without a variance
for the average. Partitioning $\{1,\dots,n\}$ into the $h$ phases
$P_j=\{j,j+h,j+2h,\dots\}$ fixes it. Within a phase, consecutive outcomes span disjoint
windows and the payoffs \emph{are} a martingale difference sequence with respect to the
thinned filtration, so each phase yields a certificate; Theorem~\ref{thm:merge} then merges
the $h$ of them with no assumption about how they depend on one another.

\begin{remark}[The valid combination is the implementable portfolio]
\label{rem:stagger}
Averaging the $h$ phase certificates means splitting capital $1/h$ across $h$ bettors, one
per daily vintage, and reading off their aggregate terminal wealth. That is precisely the
staggered portfolio the economic section adopts in Section~\ref{sec:economics}: an equal
allocation to each of the $h$ daily vintages, each held $h$ days. The statistically valid
combination and the economically natural implementation are the same object, so the
arbitrary choice of starting phase disappears from both at once.
\end{remark}

\section{How much data an alpha needs}
\label{sec:design}

Because evidence is log wealth and log wealth grows at a rate fixed by the signal's
information ratio, the sample-size question has a closed-form answer.

\begin{theorem}[Detection horizon]
\label{thm:horizon}
Let the signal-weighted strategy have per-period information ratio $\mathrm{IR}_p$, small,
and let the stake be the Kelly fraction. Then expected log wealth grows at
$\mathrm{IR}_p^2/2$ nats per period, and the expected time to reach the level-$\alpha$
threshold is
\[
  T^\ast \;=\; \frac{2\ln(1/\alpha)}{\mathrm{IR}_p^{2}}\ \text{periods}
        \;=\; \frac{2\ln(1/\alpha)}{\mathrm{IR}^{2}}\ \text{years},
\]
where $\mathrm{IR}=\mathrm{IR}_p\sqrt{P}$ is the annualised ratio at $P$ periods a year.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[sketch]
With $\psi_t=g_t\phi_t$, $\sup_\lambda\mathbb{E}\log(1+\lambda\psi)=
\mathbb{E}[\psi]^2/(2\mathbb{E}[\psi^2])+O(\mathbb{E}[\psi]^3)$. For the identity payoff
and $g_t$ proportional to the conditional mean $m_t$, $\mathbb{E}[\psi]=\mathbb{E}[m^2]$ and
$\mathbb{E}[\psi^2]=\mathbb{E}[m^2]\sigma^2$ to leading order, so the rate is
$\mathbb{E}[m^2]/(2\sigma^2)=\mathrm{IR}_p^2/2$. Rejection needs $\ln(1/\alpha)$ nats.
The full statement and the bounded-payoff case are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Six over IR squared]
\label{cor:six}
At $\alpha=0.05$, $T^\ast\approx 6/\mathrm{IR}^2$ years: six years for an information ratio
of $1.0$, twenty-four for $0.5$, sixty-seven for $0.3$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[The bound is not an artifact of this instrument]
\label{cor:optimal}
The Kullback--Leibler divergence per period between the alternative and $H_0$ is
$\mathrm{IR}_p^2/2+o(\mathrm{IR}_p^2)$. By the standard sequential lower bound, no
level-$\alpha$ anytime-valid test has expected stopping time below
$\ln(1/\alpha)/\mathrm{KL}$, so $T^\ast$ is optimal to first order and no cleverer test
recovers the missing years.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[What anytime-validity actually costs]
\label{cor:free}
Compared at matched level \emph{and matched power}, the certificate needs about twice the data
of a one-sided fixed-sample test. Log wealth at the oracle stake is approximately
$\mathcal{N}(x, 2x)$ with $x = T\,\mathrm{IR}_p^2/2$, so attaining power $\beta$ requires
$x(\beta) = \big([z_\beta\sqrt2 + \sqrt{2z_\beta^2+4\ln(1/\alpha)}\,]/2\big)^2$, against
$(z_\alpha+z_\beta)^2/2$ for the fixed-sample test. The ratio is scale-free---the information
ratio cancels---and equals $1.90$ at $\alpha=0.05,\ \beta=0.80$, $2.21$ at median power, and
stays between $1.9$ and $2.0$ for $\beta$ up to $0.95$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[A correction, and why the comparison is easy to get wrong]
\label{rem:correction}
An earlier version of this paper reported that anytime-validity costs three per cent more
data. That figure came from comparing the certificate's \emph{median crossing time},
$2\ln(1/\alpha)/\mathrm{IR}^2 = 5.99/\mathrm{IR}^2$ years, with the fixed-sample test's
\emph{eighty-per-cent-power} sample size, $6.18/\mathrm{IR}^2$. Those are not the same
quantity: the first is a median and the second is an $80$th percentile. Matched properly the
cost is $11.8/\mathrm{IR}^2$ against $6.2/\mathrm{IR}^2$. We record the error because the
$5.99$ against $6.18$ coincidence is seductive and because the corrected number changes the
advice: continuous monitoring is worth roughly a doubling of the sample, which is a real price
and one a practitioner should pay knowingly.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Not knowing your own stake is not free]
\label{cor:regret}
If the Kelly fraction must be learned online, log wealth falls short of the oracle's by a
regret $\rho_T$, with $\rho_T=\tfrac12\ln T+O(1)$ attainable and unimprovable in order. The
horizon then solves $T\,\mathrm{IR}_p^2/2=\ln(1/\alpha)+\rho_T$. At $T\approx2{,}000$ daily
periods $\rho_T\approx3.8$ nats, larger than the $3.0$ nats needed to reject: the cost of
not knowing how strong the signal is exceeds the entire evidentiary budget, and multiplies
$T^\ast$ by between two and three. Declaring in advance the smallest information ratio worth detecting fixes
the stake and removes the term.
\end{corollary}

Corollary~\ref{cor:regret} converts a methodological platitude into a power calculation. A
pre-registered effect size is not a formality here; it is worth a factor of two in data.
Section~\ref{sec:results-cert} therefore reports both variants, with the design ratio
declared before the data were touched.

shortfall cannot be argued away by a better test.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

The certificate is released as \texttt{alphacert}, a dependency-light package that consumes
walk-forward forecasts a pipeline has already written and returns the wealth path, the
anytime-valid $p$-value, the merged grid-level e-value, the e-BH selection, and the design
quantities of Section~\ref{sec:design}.

\paragraph{Cost.} Calibrating a Clark--West-family test on a machine-learning pipeline means
simulating the null and refitting the entire pipeline in every replication:
Section~\ref{sec:calibration} required $2{,}000$ refits of three assets, two horizons and
three estimators, several hours on a workstation, and it must be redone whenever the feature
set, the estimator, or the sample changes. The certificate needs none of it, because
Theorem~\ref{thm:validity} is a finite-sample statement rather than an approximation to be
checked. One pass over the existing forecasts costs $O(nG)$ for a drift grid of $G$ points,
or $O(n)$ under Lemma~\ref{lem:concave}; on this study a certificate takes about
$0.1$--$0.5$ seconds against several hours for the corresponding calibration. That gap is
the difference between an instrument that runs nightly on every live strategy and one that
occupies a cluster for a weekend.

\paragraph{The one numerical trap.} The infimum in \eqref{eq:cert} is over a continuum and is
evaluated on a grid. The grid must resolve the drift to better than its own sampling error
$\sigma/\sqrt{n}$: a coarser grid does not merely lose precision, it lifts the numerical
infimum above the true one and the process stops being conservative. On this study
$\sigma/\sqrt{n}=1.7\times10^{-3}$; at spacings of $5\times10^{-4}$, $2.5\times10^{-4}$ and
$1\times10^{-4}$ no e-value moves in the fourth decimal, and the coarsest is five times
faster. The library defaults to $10^{-4}$, exposes the spacing, and its test suite records
the failure mode of a deliberately coarse grid so that the trap is documented rather than
latent.

\paragraph{Live use.} Because the guarantee holds at every stopping time, the wealth path
can be run forward on a deployed strategy and inspected daily without any correction for
looking. Two decision rules follow directly. A strategy is \emph{certified} the first time
its wealth crosses $1/\alpha$. A research line can be \emph{retired} when the anytime-valid
ceiling of Section~\ref{sec:ceiling} falls below the trading cost hurdle---a rigorous
statement that the edge, if any, is too small to pay for, available at any time and without
a pre-committed sample size.

\subsection{Point accuracy}

Out-of-sample $\rsq$ is negative in 16 of 18 machine-learning settings, from
$-0.0908$ (ETH, $h=7$, ridge) to $+0.0031$ (BTC, $h=1$, elastic net). Rank
information coefficients lie between $-0.080$ and $+0.053$. Figure~\ref{fig:r2}
gives the full surface and Table~\ref{tab:accuracy} the underlying numbers.

That ridge attains $\mathrm{CW} = +2.62$ with $p = 0.004$ on BTC at $h=1$ while its
$\rsq$ is $-0.0079$ has two explanations, and only one of them was in the earlier
version of this paper. Clark--West does ask about \emph{population} mean squared
prediction error, and a model can have a lower population error while its own
parameter-estimation noise leaves the realised forecast worse than a constant. But
the two numbers are also computed against different benchmarks---the $p$-value against
zero, the $\rsq$ against the recursive mean---and, per
Equation~\eqref{eq:drift} and Table~\ref{tab:size}, the $p$-value comes from a test
that rejects 14\% of the time when nothing is predictable. The gap is therefore not
usable as evidence of a weak signal: both readings are available from the same
forecasts, and the second explanation requires no signal at all.

\subsection{Predictive accuracy against the random walk}
\label{sec:results-tests}

Table~\ref{tab:inference} summarises. Under Diebold--Mariano the conclusion would
be that machine learning is actively harmful here: no setting beats the random
walk and seven lose to it significantly. Under Clark--West against the same zero
benchmark, five settings reject at an uncorrected 5\%. Both numbers derive from
identical forecasts, and neither is the answer, because the second test rejects
14--30\% of the time when nothing is predictable (Table~\ref{tab:size}).

Ordered by $p$-value the five are BTC ridge ($p = 0.004$), BTC elastic net
($0.005$), BTC GBM ($0.022$), and ETH ridge ($0.031$), all at $h = 1$, together
with BTC elastic net at $h = 7$ ($0.039$). The sixth, BTC ridge at $h=7$, misses
at $p = 0.05004$; a count that turns on four parts in $10^5$ should not be
described as five.

\paragraph{The benchmark changes which settings reject, not how many.}
Substituting the recursively estimated mean---the actual slopes-zero restriction of
these estimators---moves the raw count from 5 of 18 to 7 of 18 at the same
bandwidth. It does not move the same settings. BTC ridge and elastic net at $h=1$
survive ($p = 0.004$ both). BTC at $h=7$ drops out ($p = 0.086$ and $0.061$).
And SOL, which shows no signal at all against the zero benchmark, becomes the
strongest rejecter: SOL GBM at $h=1$ goes from $p = 0.207$ to $0.031$, SOL ridge at
$h=7$ from $0.923$ to $0.022$, SOL elastic net at $h=7$ from $0.921$ to $0.011$.
ETH at $h=7$ moves further against the models.

The earlier reading of this table---four of five rejections in BTC, consistent with
a weak short-horizon effect in the most liquid asset---was an artifact of the
benchmark. Under the corrected benchmark the geography reverses.

Calibration then does something different to each count. Against the zero forecast,
5 of 18 is close to the $\approx 4$ that the sign-flipped null produces when nothing is
predictable, so the zero-benchmark rejections carry essentially no information. Against the
recursive mean, 7 of 18 is well above the $\approx 1$ expected, and Section~\ref{sec:joint}
shows by joint resampling that the excess is not accounted for by size. The two benchmarks therefore support opposite readings of
the same forecasts: under the default one the study has nothing to report, and under
the one the estimators actually nest it has a small excess to report.

We are careful about how much that excess is worth. It does not survive family-wise
control at any bandwidth, and only 2 of 18 survive false-discovery control at the
$h-1$ bandwidth (5 at the plug-in bandwidth, with 0 and 1 surviving family-wise
control respectively); since nothing in the data selects the bandwidth, the
surviving set is not identified by this design. The two false-discovery survivors
are BTC ridge and BTC elastic net at $h=1$, which with $\alpha = 10^{-3}$ are close
to the same estimator on the same data, so they are one candidate finding rather
than two. Every other measurement in this paper points the other way: those settings
have negative $\rsq$ against the same benchmark, no sign-timing skill, and no alpha
once market exposure and a feasible entry delay are accounted for.

\paragraph{Wild Clark--West changes nothing here.}
\citet{pincheira2021} restore asymptotic normality by multiplying the larger
model's error by $\theta_t \sim \mathcal{N}(1,\phi^2)$ with
$\phi = c\,\widehat{\mathrm{sd}}(\hat e^{\,m})$ and $c$ a small constant
(Appendix~\ref{sec:formulas}). Applied to these forecasts, the statistic is
numerically almost identical to Clark--West: across all 36 setting--benchmark pairs
the largest absolute difference is $0.0034$ at $c = 0.04$ and $0.0019$ at
$c = 0.02$, and no rejection decision at the 5\% level changes under any $c$ in the
authors' grid. The reason is a scale mismatch specific to this regime. The
perturbation's standard deviation is calibrated to the forecast \emph{error},
$\phi = c\,\widehat{\mathrm{sd}}(\hat e^{\,m})$, whereas the core statistic's own
scale is set by the forecast \emph{difference} $\hat y^{\,m} - \hat y^{\,b}$. Here
the second is far smaller than the first, and the injected term contributes between
$0.5\%$ and $5.1\%$ of the core statistic's standard deviation at $c = 0.02$
(median $1.5\%$), so the statistic barely moves. The single setting where the draw
matters is BTC
ridge at $h=7$, whose $p$-value is $0.05004$: there the 5\% decision goes to the
model in 43\% of draws. Separately, and more fundamentally, $\mathbb{E}[\theta_t]=1$
leaves the drift term of Equation~\eqref{eq:drift} untouched, so no version of the
statistic can repair a benchmark that encodes the wrong hypothesis.

\subsection{Certificates}
\label{sec:results-cert}

Table~\ref{tab:cert} runs the certificate of Section~\ref{sec:certificate} on the same
eighteen settings, using as signal the model's forecast minus the intercept-only forecast
from the same window and refit schedule. Nothing is refit: the certificate consumes the
forecasts the pipeline already wrote. At $h=7$ the sample is split into its seven phases and
the phase e-values are averaged, per Remark~\ref{rem:stagger}.

Nothing is certified (Figure~\ref{fig:certificate}, middle). The largest e-value in the grid
is $3.05$ against the $20$ required at $\alpha=0.05$; the average over the eighteen settings,
which is by Theorem~\ref{thm:merge} a valid test of the global null under arbitrary
dependence, is $1.43$; e-BH selects nothing. Pre-committing the stake to an annualised
information ratio of $1.0$---declared before the data were touched, as
Corollary~\ref{cor:regret} recommends---does not change the reading: the largest designed
e-value is $2.85$.

The directional variant, $\phi=\operatorname{sign}$, is the anytime-valid replacement for
the market-timing test of Section~\ref{sec:sign} and inherits none of its defects: it needs
no asymptotic variance, and its phase average requires no distribution for the average of
dependent standardised statistics. Its grid-level e-value is $1.07$, with a largest cell value of $1.29$. The sign-timing
results
this study once reported at $p\approx0.001$ leave no trace under an instrument that is
valid for the design that produced them.

Read alongside the Clark--West columns, the table is not a contradiction but a scale
statement---and, as Section~\ref{sec:headtohead} shows, partly a statement about the
certificate's own conservatism. The implied information ratios---read off the realised growth rate through
Theorem~\ref{thm:horizon}, $\mathrm{IR}=\sqrt{2P\Lambda}$---run from $0$ to $0.69$. The
smallest ratio this sample could certify is $1.59$ ($1.11$ with the stake pre-committed).
The settings Clark--West likes are the ones whose implied ratios sit closest to that floor
without reaching it. The instruments do not disagree about the data. They disagree about what six years suffices
to settle, and the honest accounting is in Section~\ref{sec:headtohead}: at these effect sizes
Clark--West is the more powerful instrument, so part of the certificate's silence is the price
of its guarantees rather than a fact about returns. What the certificate contributes here is
not a stronger verdict but a bounded one.

\subsection{What the guarantee costs: the certificate against Clark--West}
\label{sec:headtohead}

A test that is valid under weaker assumptions, at every stopping time, cannot also be more
powerful than one that assumes more and looks once. The question is the exchange rate. It is
not reported in the e-value literature for this comparison, and a reader entitled to be
sceptical will ask for it before believing anything in Section~\ref{sec:results-cert}, so we
measure it in the design that is \emph{least} favourable to us: returns i.i.d.\ with a
realistic drift and exogenous features, so that the benchmark's estimation error is
uncorrelated with the signal and Clark--West against the recursive mean is exactly correctly
sized. Measured size confirms it, at $5.5\%$.

Table~\ref{tab:headtohead} separates two losses that are easy to conflate.

\emph{The construction is nearly as efficient as the incumbent.} At matched size the
pre-committed certificate reaches $0.757$ where Clark--West reaches $0.850$ at
$\mathrm{IR}=2$, and $0.998$ against $1.000$ at $\mathrm{IR}=3$. Eliminating the drift rather
than estimating it, hedging it two-sidedly, and standardising the signal predictably together
cost surprisingly little. Two design choices in Section~\ref{sec:certificate} are responsible
for that being true rather than false: weighting the capital split $0.9/0.1$ instead of
equally, worth a quarter to a half of the power, and pre-committing the stake rather than
learning it, worth another third at $\mathrm{IR}=2$.

\emph{Anytime-validity is what actually costs.} At the nominal thresholds the gap is large
($0.480$ against $0.853$), and essentially all of it is the certificate's measured size of
$0.5$--$0.7\%$ against a nominal $5\%$. That conservatism is not a defect to be engineered
away: Ville's inequality is tight only for a process that jumps straight to the threshold, and
a wealth path built from many small bets does not. Corollary~\ref{cor:free} prices it in
advance and independently---about a factor of two in sample size---and the measured gap is of
that order.

Two negative results belong here because they were the obvious things to try. Betting
\emph{above} the Kelly fraction, on the theory that power at a fixed threshold is a
goal-reaching problem rather than a growth problem, makes matters strictly worse at every
information ratio we tried: doubling the stake takes power at $\mathrm{IR}=2$ from $0.217$ to
$0.060$. And replacing the plug-in stake with a mixture over a stake grid, which attains the
optimal $\tfrac12\ln T$ regret, buys almost nothing here ($0.257$ against $0.233$) at forty
times the computation, because the plug-in is already close to that bound.

The reading we take from the table is the one a practitioner needs. Where the recursive-mean
benchmark is defensible, the sample is fixed in advance, and one look is enough, Clark--West
is the more powerful instrument and should be used. The certificate is for the cases it cannot
serve: monitoring a live strategy, a contested or estimated benchmark, a dependent grid of
settings, a pipeline outside any asymptotic theory, and a sample whose end the analyst does
not want to have to fix in advance. It buys those, and the price is roughly a doubling of the
data.

\subsection{Positive control: can the instrument say yes?}
\label{sec:positive}

A method whose only demonstration on real data finds nothing is unfalsified in the positive
direction, and its silence is not interpretable. Before reading anything into
Table~\ref{tab:cert} we therefore point the same instrument, on the same three assets over the
same window, at structure that is known to be there.

Realised volatility is the natural control: it is strongly predictable from its own past, it
lives on the same price series, and it is not what any model in this study forecasts, so
nothing about the exercise is circular. The signal is the trailing five-day realised volatility
and the outcome is the next day's; the payoff is the identity, so only a martingale-difference
null and an a-priori envelope are assumed.

\subsection{What the features could still be worth}
\label{sec:ceiling}

Failing to certify is the less useful half of an answer. The half a desk can act on is the
anytime-valid ceiling of Section~\ref{sec:implementation}: an upper confidence bound,
uniform over time, on the incremental value $\theta=\mathbb{E}[z_t(y_t-\mu)]$ of the
standardised signal, with the drift eliminated exactly as in the certificate.

Expressed as annualised information ratios, the $95\%$ ceilings in Table~\ref{tab:cert} run
from $0.63$ to $2.55$, and every interval contains zero. The correct summary of this study
is therefore neither ``the features predict'' nor ``the features do not predict'' but a
statement with a number in it: \emph{after six years of daily data, the incremental
annualised information ratio of twelve standard technical features on these three assets
lies between $0.6$ and $2.6$ depending on the setting, and cannot be distinguished from
zero}. That is a bound a research
manager can act on---it does not clear a costed hurdle for a strategy that must also carry
market exposure---and it is a bound no $p$-value in this literature reports.

under the line; controlling the family-wise error rate leaves nothing, because the
smallest raw $p$-value, $0.0044$, does not clear the Holm threshold of
$0.05/18 = 0.0028$ (Figure~\ref{fig:mt}). Those corrections are applied to
$p$-values from a test whose size against this benchmark is 14--30\% rather than
5\%, so they do not control the error rates they name. This is not a technicality
that cuts one way: the oversized test inflates the raw $p$-values' evidential
reading, while the multiplicity adjustment is calibrated to a nominal level the test
does not have. No calibrated bound on the false-discovery rate over this family can
be extracted from the zero-benchmark column.

Against the recursive mean the adjustments are meaningful, because there the test is
approximately correctly sized. The same two settings survive false-discovery control
($p^{\mathrm{BH}} = 0.035$), none survives family-wise control, and at the plug-in
bandwidth the survivor counts are 5 and 1.

\paragraph{The count needs a joint null, not an expected value.} An earlier version compared
the observed number of rejections with the \emph{sum} of the marginal rejection
probabilities. That is $\mathbb{E}[N]$, not the distribution of $N$, and the eighteen tests
are strongly dependent: ridge and elastic net are nearly the same estimator, the horizons
share observations, the assets are cross-sectionally correlated, and every setting sees the
same regimes. Under positive dependence false rejections cluster, so $P(N\ge7)$ can be far
larger than a binomial calculation with the same mean suggests. We therefore run the
\emph{entire} eighteen-setting experiment inside each null replication---three synthetic
assets sharing a common sign process, two horizons, three estimators, all eighteen
statistics---and read the joint quantities off the resulting vectors
(\texttt{audit/scripts/mc\_joint\_null.py}). Across $2{,}000$ replications the count has mean
$0.96$ and standard deviation $1.43$, against $0.93$ for eighteen independent tests: the
dependence the reviewer of an earlier draft anticipated is real and inflates the spread by
more than half. It does not rescue the count. The observed seven sits at
$P(N\ge7)=0.0035$, with $P(N\ge5)=0.039$ and $P(N\ge6)=0.014$, and the marginal cell sizes
run from $3.5\%$ to $7.5\%$ with a median of $4.9\%$, confirming the calibration of
Table~\ref{tab:size} setting by setting. Individually, however, nothing survives: the
largest statistic, $2.667$, sits just under the null's $95$th percentile of $2.689$, so the
max-$T$ global-null $p$-value is $0.052$ and the smallest Romano--Wolf step-down adjusted
$p$-value \citep{romano2005} is also $0.052$. The honest reading is therefore split: the
\emph{number} of rejections is surprising under the joint null, and \emph{no individual
setting} is.

That is the strongest form the $p$-value evidence takes, and it is why
Section~\ref{sec:results-cert} is not a contradiction of it: the certificate agrees the
implied effect sits near the edge of what six years can resolve, and declines to call it.

\subsection{Economic performance}
\label{sec:economics}
\label{sec:results-econ}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig7_sharpe.pdf}
  \caption{Net Sharpe with 95\% circular-block-bootstrap intervals ($10{,}000$ resamples),
  under the primary specification: an equal-weighted portfolio over all $h$ daily vintages,
  entered one bar after the signal is observable. The dashed line marks buy-and-hold on the
  same schedule and cost model. No interval excludes zero.}
  \label{fig:sharpe}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{How much of an interval is the bootstrap's own noise.} Every Sharpe interval below
is a circular block bootstrap with $10{,}000$ resamples, and that count is not decorative. At
$500$ resamples---what an earlier version used---the lower bound of the 95\% interval moves by
up to $0.25$ Sharpe points across five random seeds, and for one of the eighteen settings it
crosses zero between seeds, so the reject-or-not reading was a property of the random number
generator. At $2{,}000$ the movement is $0.09$ and the flip persists; at $10{,}000$ it is
$0.042$ at worst, median $0.023$, and no setting's verdict changes with the seed. We report
the $10{,}000$-resample intervals throughout and treat anything finer than $0.05$ Sharpe
points as below the resolution of the procedure.

These are conditional intervals, and the conditioning matters. The bootstrap resamples
realised strategy returns after the forecasts and positions have been produced, so it prices
the sampling variability of the return sequence and nothing else. It does not price model
estimation, the expanding-window refits, early stopping, or the selection of a model, an
asset, a horizon, a benchmark and a bandwidth from the grid that was searched. They are
therefore intervals for the realised strategy-return sequence, not selection-adjusted evidence
of trading profitability, and they are narrower than intervals for the research procedure that
produced them would be. The certificate of Section~\ref{sec:certificate} is our answer to
that gap rather than a wider bootstrap: it is valid at every stopping time and merges across
the grid without a dependence assumption, which is what selection adjustment is for.

\paragraph{The primary specification, and why it is not the one earlier versions reported.}
Two conventions have to be fixed before a Sharpe ratio means anything, and this study got
both wrong at first. The features at bar $t$ are computed from the completed close $C_t$ and
predict the return over $(t,t+1]$, so collecting that return requires being positioned from
$C_t$---an order filled at the very price that produced the signal. And at $h=7$ there are
seven starting offsets, nothing distinguishes them, and Figure~\ref{fig:phase} shows their
Sharpe ratios spanning up to $1.40$ points within a single setting. The primary specification
therefore delays entry by one bar and allocates $1/h$ to each daily vintage. The second
choice is not merely more sensible: by Remark~\ref{rem:stagger} the staggered portfolio
\emph{is} the phase-averaged certificate, so the same object settles the statistical and the
economic question.

Table~\ref{tab:execution} prices the two conventions separately. Staggering is a no-op at
$h=1$, as it must be, and moves $h=7$ Sharpe ratios by up to $0.30$. The execution delay is
the expensive one, and only at the short horizon: it costs up to $0.74$ Sharpe at $h=1$
(ETH GBM $0.91\to0.21$, BTC ridge $0.51\to-0.23$) and at most $0.18$ at $h=7$, because a
seven-day position does not care much which day it opens. Every one-day result in the earlier
version was therefore a property of an execution convention that cannot be traded.

\paragraph{Under the feasible specification, nothing survives.} No setting out of eighteen
has a net Sharpe whose bootstrap interval excludes zero. The largest is ETH GBM at $h=7$,
$0.788$ with interval $[-0.007, 1.594]$---close enough to the line to make the point that the
line is where it is for a reason. Five settings beat their own buy-and-hold on the point
estimate, none by a margin the interval can separate from zero, and the largest deflated
Sharpe ratio in the grid is $0.715$. Mean maximum drawdown across the eighteen is $-84\%$.

\paragraph{What is left is market exposure.} Regressing each strategy's net return on
buy-and-hold's, on the same schedule and cost model, with a Newey--West standard error on the
intercept, no setting has a positive alpha distinguishable from zero: the largest $t$-statistic
on a positive alpha is $0.91$ (SOL elastic net, $h=7$). Three are significantly \emph{negative}
(ETH elastic net at $h=1$, $t=-2.60$; ETH elastic net and ridge at $h=7$, $t=-2.24$ and
$-2.15$). The pattern is the one the exposure column predicts: settings with a high percentage
long carry betas of $0.74$--$0.85$ to buy-and-hold and contribute nothing beyond it, while the
settings that actually take both sides have betas near $0.2$ and no alpha either.

\paragraph{One cost scenario is not a cost model.} The $17$ bp per side charged above is a
single assumption. Sweeping it with the forecasts held fixed gives each setting a break-even
cost, the per-side charge at which its net Sharpe reaches zero: $15$ of $18$ are positive at
zero cost, $14$ at $17$ bp, $9$ at $40$ bp, and the median break-even is $36$ bp, roughly
twice the assumed charge. The sign of the economic result is therefore not delicate at the
margin. What the sweep exposes instead is the exposure finding from another direction: the
settings that tolerate the highest costs are the ones that barely trade, and the highest
break-even in the grid, $157$ bp, belongs to a setting that is $89\%$ long. Cost tolerance
here is a function of turnover rather than of forecast quality.

A flat per-side charge is still a first-order assumption and we do not claim otherwise. It
does not scale with trade size, depth, spread, volatility, venue, or maker-versus-taker
status, and it carries no market-impact term, so it can price a small book and cannot price
capacity. What the sweep supports is the negative claim---that no setting's result is created
or destroyed by the level of the charge---and not a positive claim about scalable
profitability.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Estimating a nuisance and eliminating it are different operations.}
Diebold--Mariano is the default in applied forecast comparison and it is the wrong default
whenever the benchmark is nested. Swapping in Clark--West fixes the nesting and leaves the
hypothesis alone. Both benchmarks then dispose of the drift by plugging an estimate into the
statistic, and the estimate's error does not disappear: with the zero forecast it is the
whole drift and Proposition~\ref{prop:distortion} prices it; with the recursive mean it is
the covariance between the benchmark's estimation error and the model's signal, which is
what the residual size distortion at $h=7$ consists of. The certificate is the same
comparison with the nuisance hedged rather than estimated, and the price of that---exactly
$\ln 2$ nats, $23\%$ more data---is small next to what it buys. The operational form of the
point is a single diagnostic question: does the larger model fit an intercept, and if so,
against which of the two nulls is the reported $p$-value computed?

\paragraph{A distributional fix does not repair a hypothesis problem.}
Wild Clark--West \citep{pincheira2021} is the right tool for the problem it was built
for---the core statistic is degenerate under the null, which is why its limit is
non-standard---and it is well sized at long horizons in the authors' own designs. It cannot
help here, and Section~\ref{sec:calibration} shows that it does not: the drift term in
Equation~\eqref{eq:drift} survives the $\theta_t$ perturbation unchanged, because
$\mathbb{E}[\theta_t]=1$. Choosing the benchmark and choosing the reference distribution are
separate decisions, and only the first determines what is being tested.

\paragraph{Validity that is proved does not have to be calibrated.}
The size distortion above is invisible in code review, survives a unit-test suite that
checks every formula against hand-computed values, and is not detected by simulating a
forecast that is independent noise. What detects it is a null built by resampling the data
through the actual pipeline with actual estimated forecasts---and that experiment has to be
redone whenever the feature set, the estimator, the sample or the horizon changes, at
$2{,}000$ pipeline refits a time. Theorem~\ref{thm:validity} removes the obligation rather
than automating it. The practical consequence is not only speed: a calibration study can
only certify the pipeline it was run on, whereas a finite-sample theorem covers the pipeline
a practitioner will build next year out of components that do not exist yet.

\paragraph{Most apparent performance was market exposure, and the certificate cannot be
fooled by it.} Under the feasible specification no setting has a Sharpe interval excluding
zero and none has a positive alpha to buy-and-hold distinguishable from it; the settings with
the highest Sharpe ratios are $85$--$94\%$ long and carry betas of $0.74$--$0.85$. Under Theorem~\ref{thm:pnl} that exposure earns no
evidence at all, because the certificate's bettor is credited with no part of the drift by
construction. The exposure regression, the delayed-execution variant and the staggered
portfolio remain worth running---they say \emph{how} a strategy made its money---but they are
no longer load-bearing for the inferential claim.

\paragraph{What is left, and what the two instruments are actually disagreeing about.}
Against the correct benchmark and with size calibrated under a dependence-preserving null,
Clark--West rejects in seven of eighteen settings against about one expected, and a joint
resampling of the whole experiment puts $P(N\ge7)$ at $0.0035$. Under certificates nothing is
certified, the grid-level e-value is $1.43$ against the $20$ required, and the implied
information ratios run from $0$ to $0.69$ against a certifiable floor of $1.59$.

It would be convenient to read the second as overturning the first. It does not, and
Section~\ref{sec:headtohead} is why. In the design where Clark--West is correctly sized it is
the more powerful instrument by a wide margin---at an information ratio of $2$, power $0.85$
against the certificate's $0.48$ at nominal thresholds---so a good part of the certificate's
silence is its own conservatism rather than the data's. Corollary~\ref{cor:optimal} does not
rescue the certificate here either: it bounds \emph{anytime-valid} procedures, and Clark--West
is not one, so its greater power is entirely consistent with the corollary. Any reading in
which the e-value simply wins is unavailable to us.

What survives is narrower and, we think, more useful. Clark--West's count is jointly
surprising and individually not: the smallest Romano--Wolf adjusted $p$-value is $0.052$, so
no particular setting is identified even by the instrument that likes them best. The two
settings it likes most have negative $\rsq$, no sign-timing skill, no alpha net of exposure,
and no Sharpe interval excluding zero. And the certificate contributes the number that neither
$p$-value can: an anytime-valid ceiling that bounds the features' incremental annualised
information ratio at $2.6$ and cannot separate it from zero. A desk can act on a ceiling. It
cannot act on a jointly-surprising-but-individually-unidentified count of seven.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Each item below is stated with what it would change if it were repaired, because a
limitation that does not say that is a hedge.

\paragraph{Limitations that bound the reported results.}
The size calibration of Section~\ref{sec:calibration} resamples all three assets' return
series, so size is established for those return distributions and not for other frequencies or
estimators outside the three used here; the booster is calibrated at $h=1$ only. The sign-flipped null removes conditional mean
predictability while preserving the volatility path, which is the dependence that
matters most for daily returns, but it does not reproduce every feature of the real
series---skewness dynamics and leverage effects, for instance, are destroyed with
the sign. The minimum detectable effect is computed but large, so little here
bounds predictability from above: a genuine $\rsq$ of, say, $+0.002$ would be
entirely consistent with what we observe: the minimum detectable effect measured in
Section~\ref{sec:calibration} is a population $\rsq$ of $1.34\%$ at 80\% power, so this
design is blind to anything an order of magnitude smaller. The multiplicity family of 18 covers one
statistic over one grid, and the hyperparameters were fixed by hand and never tuned,
so every conclusion is about $\mathrm{Ridge}(\alpha{=}1)$,
$\mathrm{ElasticNet}(10^{-3}, 0.5)$, and one booster configuration rather than about
a model family; a tuned search would need its own multiplicity accounting, which
would raise the bar rather than lower it. The sample end date was unpinned until
this revision---the default configuration resolved it to the current day, so any run
missing the committed cache silently re-dated the study---and the consequence was not
hypothetical: one Monte Carlo pass was measured on a sample twelve bars longer than
the manuscript's and reported cell values differing by up to five percentage points.
\texttt{StudyConfig.end} is now pinned to 2026-07-18 in the configuration rather than
only by the presence of the cache.

\paragraph{Limitations of the certificate.} Three are worth naming and none is hidden by the
theorem. \emph{The assumption is not free.} The default payoff needs the outcome to be
conditionally symmetric about its drift; that permits arbitrary volatility clustering and tail
thickness but excludes systematically skewed conditional distributions, and daily
cryptocurrency returns are mildly skewed. It is also refutable from the marginal
distribution alone, and daily cryptocurrency returns refute it: the sample skewness is
$-1.05$ on BTC, $-0.96$ on ETH and $-0.23$ on SOL, each rejecting symmetry at
$p<10^{-4}$. For that reason the identity payoff, which needs only a martingale difference
plus an a-priori envelope, is the default and is what Table~\ref{tab:cert} reports; the
bounded payoffs are offered for series where symmetry is defensible and this one is not. The
$\tanh$ variant, run anyway, gives a largest e-value of $1.81$ and a grid-level $1.18$
against the identity's $3.05$ and $1.43$: different numbers, same conclusion, and the weaker
assumption happens also to be the stronger result here. \emph{The power is real
but bounded.} Corollary~\ref{cor:optimal} says no anytime-valid test does better in order, but
a fixed-sample test that is pre-committed, looked at once, and handed the true drift needs
$23\%$ less data; a study that genuinely will not look at its data until a pre-registered
sample size is reached gives up nothing by using one. \emph{The infimum is numerical.} The
drift search is a grid, and a grid too coarse to resolve $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ silently destroys
the guarantee rather than merely weakening it. We report the refinement check, the library
defaults conservatively and exposes the spacing, and its test suite records the failure mode;
that is mitigation, not elimination.

\paragraph{Limitations that bound generalisation.}
Asset selection is not survivorship-free (Section~\ref{sec:data}); this biases
towards finding profitable long exposure, which strengthens a negative result about
predictability and would invalidate any cross-sectional claim. We use daily spot
bars only, with no order book, funding rates, intraday structure, or cross-exchange
information, and short-horizon predictability, if it exists, is more likely to live
at frequencies this data cannot resolve---so the negative result should be read as
being about daily bars specifically. Short positions are modelled as fully
collateralised, returning $-r$ with no borrow cost, margin, or liquidation, which is
optimistic; correcting it would lower the reported Sharpe ratios and cannot raise
them. Costs are a flat 17 bp per side that does not scale with size or volatility; we report a
break-even sweep in Section~\ref{sec:results-econ} but not a queue-level execution model. Intervals are percentile bootstrap
rather than BCa, adequate for a reject-or-not reading and mildly biased for a
statistic as asymmetric as the Sharpe ratio. The deflated Sharpe ratio deflates for
the six models raced within one asset--horizon---three of which are
benchmarks---rather than for the whole grid or for the choices fixed before the grid
was run, so it is an upper bound on the true corrected figure. Tightening it would
not change a conclusion: the largest deflated Sharpe among the 18 settings is
$0.847$ (BTC GBM, $h=7$), already short of any conventional threshold.

\section{Conclusion}

Tests of out-of-sample predictability dispose of the asset's drift by estimating it, and
that is where they break. Proposition~\ref{prop:distortion} makes the failure exact: against
a zero benchmark the Clark--West statistic converges to the $t$-statistic of the asset's mean
return, so on an asset with Bitcoin's drift a model containing no features at all is declared
significantly predictive in $43\%$ of samples, and with twelve estimated coefficients it is
still $12\%$. Replacing zero with the recursive mean removes the leading term and leaves the
benchmark's own estimation error behind.

The alternative developed here does not estimate the drift. A certificate is a non-negative
wealth process---one bettor staking on the signal, one hedging every candidate drift---whose
infimum over the nuisance is bounded by a martingale under the entire composite null.
Validity is finite-sample and holds at every stopping time, with no bandwidth, no long-run
variance, no bootstrap, no asymptotics and no refitting, for arbitrary black-box pipelines.
Three things follow that a $t$-statistic cannot supply: the process may be monitored daily on
a live strategy without any correction for looking; its logarithm is the profit and loss of
an explicit drift-hedged strategy, so a $5\%$ rejection \emph{is} a twentyfold multiplication
of capital that was credited with none of the market's return; and because e-values average
under arbitrary dependence, an eighteen-setting grid needs no joint bootstrap and an
overlapping $h$-step forecast is combined by splitting capital across its $h$ daily
vintages---the staggered portfolio, arrived at from the statistics rather than bolted on
afterwards.

The construction also closes the sample-size question. Certifying an annualised information
ratio $\mathrm{IR}$ takes $2\ln(1/\alpha)/\mathrm{IR}^2$ years, about $6/\mathrm{IR}^2$ at
the $5\%$ level, rising to $7.4/\mathrm{IR}^2$ once the drift hedge is paid for and to
$16/\mathrm{IR}^2$ if the stake is learned online rather than declared in advance. The bound
is optimal to first order, so no better test recovers the missing years, and continuous
monitoring costs about twice the data of a fixed-sample test that may be looked at once. Six years of daily data---about what any liquid cryptocurrency offers---cannot certify
an information ratio below $1.59$.

Applied to this study, nothing is certified. The grid-level e-value is $1.18$ against the
$20$ required, the directional variant that replaces an invalid market-timing test gives
$1.07$, and the implied information ratios run from $0$ to $0.50$. We do not read that as
evidence of no predictability, and the instrument says why: the settings Clark--West likes
imply ratios just below what six years can resolve. The reportable number is therefore not a
$p$-value but a ceiling---after six years, the incremental information ratio of twelve
standard technical features on these three assets is at most $2.6$, and cannot be
distinguished from zero. That is a statement a research manager can act on, and it is
available at any time, on any pipeline, for the cost of one pass over forecasts that have
already been computed.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} All results, figures, and tables in this paper are
generated by a single command from a public repository, and the tables reproduced
here are emitted directly from the committed results file rather than transcribed.
The test suite covers the leakage guarantees and checks the statistical formulas
against hand-computed reference values. It did not, and could not, detect the
benchmark misspecification of Section~\ref{sec:cw}: every formula was implemented
correctly and every test passed. The size calibration of
Section~\ref{sec:calibration}, the corrected-benchmark re-test, the Wild Clark--West
implementation, the non-overlapping sign tests, the exposure regressions, and the
delayed-execution backtest are separate scripts under \texttt{audit/}, each
reproducing the numbers quoted for it, together with a full record of which earlier
claims they overturn. The per-observation forecasts on which every test in this
paper is computed are committed as a file, so the inference can be re-analysed
without re-running the models. Two cautions apply to anyone repeating the size
experiment. It must be run with the sample end date pinned, for the reason given in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}; and it needs a serious replication count, because at
400 replications we observed cell-to-cell movement of up to three percentage points
between random seeds. The certificate needs neither caution, because it needs no size
experiment; the one thing it does need is a drift grid fine enough to resolve the mean, and
the library's default and its test suite both enforce that.

\section{Proofs and derivations}
\label{app:proofs}

\paragraph{Theorem~\ref{thm:horizon}, in full.} Let $\psi_t=g_t\phi_t(\mu_0)$ with
$\mathbb{E}[\psi_t\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\eta_t$ under the alternative. For a stake
$\lambda$ small enough that $\lambda|\psi|\le c<1$,
\[
  \mathbb{E}\log(1+\lambda\psi)
  = \lambda\,\mathbb{E}[\psi] - \tfrac{\lambda^2}{2}\mathbb{E}[\psi^2] + O(\lambda^3),
\]
maximised at $\lambda^\ast=\mathbb{E}[\psi]/\mathbb{E}[\psi^2]$ with value
$\Lambda=\mathbb{E}[\psi]^2/(2\mathbb{E}[\psi^2])+O(\mathbb{E}[\psi]^3)$. Take $\phi$ the
identity, $s_t\equiv\sigma$, and $g_t\propto m_t$ where $m_t=\mathbb{E}[y_t\mid
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]-\mu_0$ is the conditional mean. Then $\mathbb{E}[\psi]\propto
\mathbb{E}[m^2]/\sigma$ and $\mathbb{E}[\psi^2]\propto\mathbb{E}[m^2]$ to leading order in
$\mathbb{E}[m^2]/\sigma^2$, so
\[
  \Lambda \;=\; \frac{\mathbb{E}[m^2]}{2\sigma^2}\big(1+o(1)\big)
           \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{IR}_p^{2}}{2}\big(1+o(1)\big),
\]
because the strategy holding $m_t/\sigma$ units earns $\mathbb{E}[m^2]/\sigma$ per period
with standard deviation $\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[m^2]}$, hence per-period information ratio
$\mathrm{IR}_p=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[m^2]}/\sigma$. Crossing $\log(1/\alpha)$ therefore takes
$T^\ast=2\log(1/\alpha)/\mathrm{IR}_p^2$ periods. For a bounded odd payoff the same
calculation runs with $\phi$ in place of the identity and multiplies $\Lambda$ by
$\mathbb{E}[\phi'(\varepsilon/\sigma)]^2/\mathbb{E}[\phi(\varepsilon/\sigma)^2]$, which for
$\phi=\tanh$ and Gaussian $\varepsilon$ is $0.98$: the bounded transform costs about two per
cent of the growth rate and buys the removal of the envelope, which for daily returns is
worth a factor of two in the other direction.

\paragraph{Corollary~\ref{cor:optimal}.} Under the alternative with conditional mean shift
$m_t$ and Gaussian innovations, the per-period Kullback--Leibler divergence from $H_0$ is
$\mathbb{E}[m^2]/(2\sigma^2)=\mathrm{IR}_p^2/2$. Wald's identity for a level-$\alpha$
sequential test with vanishing type-II error gives
$\mathbb{E}[\tau]\ge\log(1/\alpha)/\mathrm{KL}$, which is $T^\ast$. Hence the constant in
Theorem~\ref{thm:horizon} is not improvable by a different anytime-valid construction.

\paragraph{Corollary~\ref{cor:regret}.} With a stake grid
$\{\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_G\}$ and uniform prior, $\tfrac1G\sum_j\prod_t(1+\lambda_j\psi_t)
\ge \tfrac1G\max_j\prod_t(1+\lambda_j\psi_t)$, so log wealth is within $\log G$ of the best
fixed stake in hindsight; a continuous mixture in the style of \citet{cover1991} attains
$\tfrac12\log T+O(1)$, and no online rule does better in order. Substituting into
$T\mathrm{IR}_p^2/2=\log(1/\alpha)+\rho_T$ and solving gives the ``learned online'' column
of Table~\ref{tab:horizon}.

\paragraph{Why the recursive mean does not finish the job.} With $b_t=\hat\mu_t$ the
Clark--West differential is $\hat f_t=2(y_t-\hat\mu_t)g_t$ with $g_t=\hat y^{\,m}_t-\hat\mu_t$,
so
\[
  \mathbb{E}[\hat f_t]
  = 2\,\mathbb{E}\big[(y_t-\mu)g_t\big] - 2\,\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat\mu_t-\mu)g_t\big].
\]
The first term is the estimand. The second is the covariance between the benchmark's own
estimation error and the model's signal, both functions of the same training window; it does
not vanish in finite samples, it grows with the horizon because overlapping labels make
$\hat\mu_t$ and $g_t$ share more observations, and it is what the residual $7$--$8\%$ size at
$h=7$ in Table~\ref{tab:size} consists of. Equation~\eqref{eq:cert} removes it because no
estimate of $\mu$ appears anywhere in the construction.

\section{Audit trail of corrected claims}
\label{app:audit}

Earlier versions of this study reported findings that did not survive their own robustness
checks. They are listed here rather than in the body, because the scientific content is in
the current sections and a reader who wants the forensic record should be able to find it in
one place. The full changelog, with the script that produced each number, is in the
repository.

\begin{description}
  \item[Benchmark.] ``Five of 18 settings reject, against $0.9$ expected from a search of
    that size.'' The comparison assumed the test was correctly sized for the hypothesis
    intended. It was not: against the zero forecast the measured rejection rate on data with
    no predictability is $14$--$21\%$ at $h=1$, so the correct expectation was about four.
    Proposition~\ref{prop:distortion} now gives the distortion in closed form.
  \item[The nesting claim.] The manuscript asserted that the zero forecast is the
    slopes-zero restriction of the estimators. It is not; the restriction is the
    training-window mean, because the intercept is unpenalised.
  \item[The validating simulation.] An earlier calibration replaced the larger model's
    forecast with exogenous noise. That construction makes the null of equal accuracy false
    rather than true and estimates no coefficient, so it measures neither size nor bias. It
    was replaced by refitting the entire pipeline inside each replication.
  \item[Sign timing.] Significant market-timing results at $p\approx0.001$ came from
    treating overlapping seven-day labels as independent. On non-overlapping subsamples they
    are $p\approx0.24$--$0.27$. The phase-averaged repair introduced at that point was
    itself invalid---it referred an average of dependent standardised statistics to a
    standard normal without deriving a variance for the average---and is superseded by the
    directional certificate of Section~\ref{sec:results-cert}.
  \item[Execution and schedule.] The primary backtest entered at the same close its features
    were computed from, which is infeasible, and reported one of $h$ arbitrary starting
    phases. Both are replaced: execution is delayed by one bar and the strategy is the
    staggered portfolio over all $h$ vintages, which Remark~\ref{rem:stagger} shows is also
    the statistically correct combination.
  \item[Size measurement.] The first Monte Carlo used $400$ replications against a sample
    that a working-directory bug had silently re-downloaded twelve bars longer. Cells moved
    by up to three percentage points between seeds. Rebuilt at $2{,}000$ replications against
    the pinned cache.
  \item[The dependence null.] A $28$--$43\%$ rejection rate under block resampling was read
    as size failure. Block resampling preserves whatever genuine within-block predictability
    the real series contains, so that rate is an upper bound on size, not a measurement of
    it. The sign-flipped null separates the two.
  \item[Novelty.] An earlier version presented the benchmark correction as its main
    contribution. A literature check established that reporting both benchmarks is standard
    practice in this literature \citep{magner2022, moosa2016, welch2008, campbell2008}. The
    contribution was narrowed accordingly, and the present version claims novelty only for
    the instrument of Sections~\ref{sec:certificate}--\ref{sec:design}.
\end{description}

Each of these was found by a check the study already ran and then reported honestly, which
is the only reason they are known. The pattern worth extracting is that none of them was
visible in the code: every one was a mismatch between a quantity the code computed correctly
and the sentence it was used to support.


\section{Statistical definitions}
\label{sec:formulas}

\paragraph{Pesaran--Timmermann.} With $P$ the hit rate, $P_y$ and $P_x$ the
proportions of positive outcomes and forecasts, and
$P_{\ast} = P_y P_x + (1-P_y)(1-P_x)$,
%
\begin{equation}
  S = \frac{P - P_{\ast}}
  {\sqrt{\widehat{\operatorname{var}}(P) - \widehat{\operatorname{var}}(P_{\ast})}}
  \;\xrightarrow{\;d\;}\; \mathcal{N}(0,1),
  \qquad
  \widehat{\operatorname{var}}(P) = \frac{P_{\ast}(1 - P_{\ast})}{n},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\operatorname{var}}(P_{\ast}) = \frac{(2P_y-1)^2 P_x (1-P_x)}{n}
  + \frac{(2P_x-1)^2 P_y (1-P_y)}{n}
  + \frac{4 P_y P_x (1-P_y)(1-P_x)}{n^2}.
\end{equation}
%
Every term divides by $n$, so $n$ must count independent observations. Section
\ref{sec:results-signs} reports the statistic on non-overlapping subsamples for
that reason.

\paragraph{Probabilistic and deflated Sharpe ratio.} With skewness $\gamma_3$ and
kurtosis $\gamma_4$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathrm{PSR}}(\mathrm{SR}^{\ast}) = \Phi\!\left[
  \frac{(\widehat{\mathrm{SR}} - \mathrm{SR}^{\ast})\sqrt{n-1}}
  {\sqrt{1 - \gamma_3 \widehat{\mathrm{SR}}
  + \frac{\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}^{2}}}\right],
\end{equation}
%
and the deflated Sharpe ratio is $\widehat{\mathrm{PSR}}$ evaluated at
$\mathrm{SR}^{\ast} = \mathbb{E}[\max_{i \le N}\mathrm{SR}_i]$, approximated by
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{i \le N} \mathrm{SR}_i\right] \approx
  \sigma_{\mathrm{SR}}\!\left[(1-\gamma)\,\Phi^{-1}\!\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{N}\right)
  + \gamma\,\Phi^{-1}\!\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{Ne}\right)\right],
\end{equation}
%
with $\gamma$ the Euler--Mascheroni constant.

\paragraph{Wild Clark--West.} Let $\hat e^{\,b}_t = y_t - \hat y^{\,b}_t$ and
$\hat e^{\,m}_t = y_t - \hat y^{\,m}_t$. \citet{pincheira2021} replace the
Clark--West core statistic $\hat f_t = 2\hat e^{\,b}_t(\hat e^{\,b}_t - \hat
e^{\,m}_t)$ with
%
\begin{equation}
  \hat f^{\,\mathrm{W}}_t
  = \hat e^{\,b}_t\!\left(\hat e^{\,b}_t - \theta_t\, \hat e^{\,m}_t\right),
  \qquad
  \theta_t \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(1, \phi^2),
  \label{eq:wcw}
\end{equation}
%
with $\theta_t$ independent of the data. Under the null the two models coincide,
so $\mathbb{E}[\hat f^{\,\mathrm{W}}_t] = \mathbb{E}[(\hat e^{\,b}_t)^2
(1-\theta_t)] = 0$ while the variance stays strictly positive, which restores the
rank condition of \citet{west1996} and with it asymptotic normality. We set
$\phi = c\,\widehat{\mathrm{sd}}(\hat e^{\,m})$ with $c \in \{0.01, 0.02, 0.04\}$
and average $K = 2$ independent draws as the authors prescribe, dividing by
$\sqrt{\sum_i\sum_j \rho_{ij}}$ where $\rho_{ij}$ is the sample correlation
between the $i$th and $j$th core sequences.
